In thirty-one years behind the wheel, I have been followed exactly once.

It sat in my mirrors for three hundred miles. All the way across the bottom of Wyoming, in the dark. Never closer. Never farther. When I slowed down, it slowed down. When I pulled off for fuel, it pulled off too.

And at three in the morning, in a ground blizzard, it finally pulled up alongside me. Close enough that I could see right into its cab.

The dash lights were on. The steering wheel was moving, just a little, the way it does when somebody's holding a lane in the wind.

And in the driver's seat, there was—

Hey. It's Hank.

Tonight I'm at a truck stop in western Nebraska. There's a pumpkin on the counter inside, with a face drawn on it in marker. Tomorrow morning I cross into Wyoming. I always get a little quiet the night before Wyoming. By the end of tonight, you'll know why.

Last time I told you about an old mechanic named Virgil Tate and a cigar box full of logbook pages. Tonight is the night I stopped driving alone.

Pull up a chair. And maybe leave a light on.

It was Thursday, October twenty-eighth, two thousand four. About eight-thirty at night. I was twenty-seven, and I'd been running coast to coast for six years.

I had forty-two thousand pounds of Washington apples behind me, in a refrigerated trailer, headed for a grocery warehouse in Ohio.

Ohio meant home. And home meant a promise. My boy was six, and that year he'd decided he was going to be a trucker for Halloween. My old cap, one of my flannel shirts down to his knees, and a steering wheel my wife cut out of a pizza box. Halloween was Sunday. I'd told him I'd be there to walk him up and down our street.

You don't break a promise like that to a six-year-old. Not if you want to keep being his dad.

I'd come down from the Northwest, and in Utah, at the bottom of Echo Canyon, I turned east onto Interstate eighty. Next stop, Wyoming.

If you've never driven Wyoming, let me tell you about it. Truckers call it the Snow Chi Minh Trail. That's not a compliment.

It's high and it's empty. You can go an hour without seeing a house light. And it's got the wind. Wyoming wind doesn't blow. It shoves. It'll lay an empty trailer on its side like a cereal box.

And out there, it doesn't have to be snowing to be a blizzard. The wind picks up the snow that's already on the ground and throws it across the road, waist high, chest high. They call it a ground blizzard. You can look straight up and see stars. And you can look straight ahead and not see your own hood.

Here's the other thing you need to know. At night, truckers talk with their lights.

When a truck passes you, he can't see where his trailer ends. So when he's clear, you flash your headlights once. That means, come on over, you've got room. And when he moves over in front of you, he says thank you by blinking his marker lights. The little amber lights along the top of the cab. Off. On. Thank you.

Remember that. It matters later.

I picked him up right there, at the junction in Echo Canyon.

A set of headlights swung onto eighty behind me and settled in about a quarter mile back. High headlights, set wide. A truck. And the left headlight was yellow. Old yellow, like the sealed-beam lights they put on trucks in the seventies. The right one was bright white. Like a man with one bad eye.

A lot of drivers like company at night. I didn't think much of it.

But after an hour, I started to notice.

He stayed a quarter mile back. Exactly. Coming up the long grade past Evanston, I dropped to forty-five, and he dropped to forty-five. On the far side I let her run up to sixty-eight, and he came with me. Like we were tied together.

So I picked up my CB.

"Eastbound, the one on my back door. You got your ears on?"

Nothing. Just the squelch.

"Eastbound. The one with the yellow headlight. Come back."

Nothing.

A trucker who doesn't want to talk, fine. But one who won't even key up and tell you to leave him alone? I'd never met one.

I thought about a calm voice on channel nineteen, three years before, in an ice storm in Missouri. I'd have given just about anything to hear it that night.

The radio stayed quiet.

Around eleven, I pulled off for fuel at a big truck stop in Rock Springs.

I watched him in my mirror all the way down the ramp. He took the exit too.

I pulled up to pump three, under the lights. He went around behind the building, to the last fuel island. Pump eight. The one at the very end, where half the lights were burned out. He stopped there in the dark. All I could see were those two headlights.

I fueled, then went inside for a printed receipt, because I write down every gallon I ever buy. I have since I was nineteen.

The cashier, a big kid in a hunting jacket, looked out toward the back island and said, "You running with the one on eight?"

I said no.

"He's been sitting out there ten minutes," the kid said. "Hasn't come in."

My cell phone had one bar in Rock Springs and none anywhere else in Wyoming, so I called home from the pay phone. My wife picked up half asleep. Our boy had worn his costume to bed, she said. Cap and all. He wanted to know if my truck was going fast enough.

I told her it was. I didn't tell her about the headlights. Maybe because saying it out loud would make it something.

When I came back outside, pump eight was empty.

No truck. No taillights. Nothing.

I got back on the interstate. And a mile down, there he was. Sitting on the shoulder past the on-ramp, lights on. Waiting. When I went by, he pulled out and settled in a quarter mile behind me. Exactly.

I typed a message to my dispatcher, Marlene, on the little satellite box on my dash. It only did text. Truck following me since Utah. Won't answer the radio.

Ten minutes later, the box beeped.

Get his number hon. Don't you stop for him.

But I never got a look at his door. All I ever saw were those two headlights.

Past Rock Springs, the road crosses the Great Divide Basin. The Continental Divide splits in two out there and goes around it, and any rain that falls in between never reaches an ocean. It just stays.

I remember thinking that around one in the morning, with those lights in my mirror. Some things that come down out there never leave.

Past Rawlins, the wind came up.

The whole trailer leans first. Then the wind hits the side of the box like a big flat hand, and the wheel jumps in your fingers. Then the snow came across the road. Not falling. Flying sideways, low, hissing over the pavement like sand.

By the time I passed the dark hump of Elk Mountain, I was in a ground blizzard. Stars overhead. And in front of me, nothing but white.

I put my flashers on and slowed to twenty-five.

And I'll tell you what I told myself. That truck behind me was some new driver, scared to death, hanging on my taillights because they were the only thing he could see. That's why he'd stayed so close all night.

That made sense. I held on to it.

My father had a rule. I can still hear it in his voice. Always leave room to stop. Whatever's in front of you, leave yourself room to stop before you hit it.

So in that whiteout, I left room. As much as my eyes could give me.

I didn't know it yet, but that rule was about to matter more than it ever had.

At about three in the morning, the headlights in my mirror started getting bigger.

For the first time in three hundred miles, he was closing in.

My stomach went cold. In a whiteout, the thing that kills you isn't always in front of you. It's the truck behind you that never sees you. I pulled as far right as I dared. And those lights kept coming. One yellow. One white.

Then they swung out into the left lane.

He came up alongside me slow. His bumper, his hood, then his cab, right next to mine. Two trucks crawling side by side through the white.

And I looked over.

The dash lights were on. The wheel was moving. Small, careful corrections, the way your hands move in a crosswind. The curtain to the sleeper was pulled back, and the bunk was made up neat.

And there was nobody in the seat.

Nobody at the wheel. Nobody in the bunk. Just an old army blanket on the passenger seat, folded in a square. The way you'd fold it for a dog to lie on.

I've had twenty-two years to come up with explanations. The driver was lying down. He wasn't. The bunk was empty. My own reflection in his glass. It wasn't. My dome light was off. Cruise control, then. Sure. But cruise control doesn't steer. Somebody has to hold that wheel in a Wyoming wind.

And somebody was. I watched it move.

Then the wind dropped. Just for a second. And it was so quiet, I could hear his engine through two sets of glass. An old engine, with a little tick in it. Like a clock.

Then he pulled ahead.

And I did the thing you do. The thing I'd done ten thousand times. I flashed my headlights once. You're clear. Come on over.

He moved over in front of me. And his marker lights blinked. Off. On.

Thank you.

Then his taillights went into the white, and they were gone.

I'd like to tell you I was brave. I wasn't. My hands were shaking so bad I had to hold the wheel at the bottom. I kept crawling forward, staring at the place where those taillights had gone.

Maybe two minutes later, something was standing in my lane.

Right in the middle of the road, in the blowing snow. Low to the ground. Two green eyes.

A dog.

I hit the brakes. And because I'd left room, the way my father taught me, the truck stopped. Forty-two thousand pounds, on snow, maybe ten feet short of that dog.

He didn't run. He just stood there in the wind with his ears flat, looking right at me through the windshield. Like he'd been waiting for me in particular.

So let me ask you something.

It's three in the morning, on the emptiest road in America. A truck with nobody in it just drove past you, said thank you, and disappeared. And now there's a dog standing in the road, looking at you like he knows your name.

Do you open that door?

I sat there a full minute. Then I got my coat and my flashlight, set my triangles out behind the trailer, and walked up front.

The wind about took me off my feet. The second I got close, the dog trotted to the right shoulder and stopped at the very edge, where the ground falls away. He looked down. Then he looked back at me. And he barked. Once.

I shined my light where he was looking.

Maybe sixty feet below the road, past a snow fence, in a dip you'd never see from the highway, there was a truck. On its side. Half buried in a drift. No lights. Snow piled against the cab like it had been there for hours.

I called on channel nine, the emergency channel. Out there, at three in the morning, channel nine was mostly hope. So I went to nineteen, and by some miracle a westbound driver heard me. He had one bar on his phone, and he called it in.

Then I went down, half sliding, with the dog right beside me.

The driver's door was facing the sky. I climbed up, wiped the snow off the window, and shined my light in.

He was an older man. Gray hair, a jean jacket, his seat belt still on. Sitting with his hands in his lap, like a man waiting for a light to change. He wasn't hurt that I could see. He was just gone.

I talked to him anyway. I told him help was coming. I told him his dog was okay. I don't know who that was for.

The trooper got there a little before five. A tall, quiet man with frost in his mustache. He looked at the snow below the driver's door. There was a circle there, packed down flat, about the size of a dog. Something had been lying there for hours. Keeping somebody company.

"He went off last evening," the trooper said. "Before the wind came up. Engine's stone cold. I'd say seven o'clock."

Seven o'clock.

At seven o'clock, I was still a long way back in Utah. I hadn't even turned onto Interstate eighty yet.

The trooper thought maybe it was his heart. That he was gone before the truck ever left the road. I hope that's true.

Then he brushed the snow off the front of the truck to read the number. And his light hit the headlights.

The left one was yellow.

The right one was white.

His name was Wendell. Wendell Rusk. Sixty-eight years old. He ran by himself, the trooper said. Just him and the dog.

In the patrol car, the trooper went through Wendell's papers. And he got real quiet. Then he handed me a fuel receipt. It had been on the clip on Wendell's visor, right where you're supposed to keep it.

The truck stop in Rock Springs. Pump eight. October twenty-eighth, two thousand four. Eleven-oh-four at night.

I took out my own receipt. Same truck stop. Pump three. Eleven-oh-one at night.

Three minutes apart. Four hours after that truck went into the snow.

An old mechanic in Ohio taught me that when the paper and the story don't agree, it's the story that's lying. Never the paper. I've believed that my whole life. And I have never wanted so bad for a piece of paper to be lying.

The trooper put the receipt back in the bag. Then he said, "I'm going to write that you saw the dog from the road." I said that was fine. That part was true too.

The dog wouldn't leave. Not when the wrecker came at daylight. Not when they brought Wendell up. He just sat at the edge of the road with snow on his back. A big, rough, brown dog with a white chest, one ear up and one ear down. One tag on his collar. On the front, it said Buddy.

The trooper said the county would come for him. Then he looked at me, and at the dog, and said, "Or not."

I opened my passenger door. I didn't call him. I just opened it.

Buddy looked down at the draw one more time. Then he jumped up into my cab like he'd done it a thousand times. I took the old blanket off my bunk and folded it in a square on the seat, and he lay down on it.

I slept that day in Laramie, with Buddy across my feet. And I made it home Sunday at four in the afternoon. Two hours before trick-or-treat. My boy came running out in my old cap with his pizza-box steering wheel, and stopped dead in the driveway, because there was a dog looking at him out of my window.

He said, "Is he ours?"

I said, "I think we're his."

Buddy rode with me for the next eleven years. On that seat, on that folded blanket, every mile. That's a story of its own, and one night I'll tell it to you. Including what was written on the back of his tag.

Here's what I think about, all these years later. For three hundred miles, I was scared of that truck. I tried to get its number. And it never came one foot closer. Not until the moment it had to.

Not everything behind you is chasing you.

To this day, when a truck sits on my back door too long at night, I don't speed up. I slow down. And I look for a dog.

And if you ever get caught in a whiteout, don't follow taillights. The man in front of you might be following the ditch. Put your flashers on, slow down, and get off at the first exit you can. And leave room to stop. Always.

Tomorrow I cross into Wyoming. Sometime after dark, I'll pass Elk Mountain. And at a dip in the ground where you'd never see a thing from the highway, I'll flash my headlights once.

You're clear. Come on over.

I've done it every time for twenty-two years. Nothing's ever blinked back.

I'm not sure what I'd do if something did.

Next time is a Sunday story. Tennessee, two thousand three. The night I was ready to quit this job for good. At two in the morning I called my father from a pay phone to tell him I was done. What he said is the reason I'm still out here.

Before you go, let me ask you something. Has an animal ever picked you, instead of the other way around? Tell me about it. I read every one.

Drive safe out there. And leave room to stop.
