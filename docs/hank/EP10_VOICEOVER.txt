There's a check in my glove box for ten thousand dollars.

It's made out to me. Not by name. It says, Pay to the order of the driver who brings my boy home.

It's dated February twentieth, nineteen fifty-six. Twenty-one years before I was born.

I've never cashed it. I couldn't if I wanted to. The bank it's drawn on closed in nineteen sixty-one.

An old man handed it to me in an envelope, at the end of a gravel road in Montana. Then he picked up his suitcase, and he walked into a house that nobody had lived in since—

Hey. It's Hank.

Tonight I'm at a truck stop in Billings, Montana. I've been parked here a lot of nights over the years, but there's one night here I've never told anybody about. Not my wife. Not my dispatcher. You're the first.

Last time I told you about the night I almost quit, and a green Kenworth parked next to my truck at sunrise. A lot of you told me who you'd call at two in the morning. I read every one.

Tonight's a different kind of story. If you're listening in the dark, I won't tell you what to do about that.

Pull up a chair.

It was Saturday, September thirteenth, two thousand eight. I was thirty-one years old. I'd been running long haul for ten years, and for almost four of them, I'd had a partner. A big, rough, brown dog named Buddy, with a white chest and one ear that never did stand up. He rode on my passenger seat, on an old army blanket folded in a square. If you've been here a while, you know how he got there.

I'd delivered a load of steel pipe in Billings that morning, and my dispatcher had nothing for me until Monday. So I had a weekend to kill, in an empty truck, in a truck stop parking lot.

Now, I need to tell you about a rule.

Almost every trucking company in America has a sticker on the passenger door, or a line in the handbook, that says the same thing. No riders. You don't pick up hitchhikers. You don't give a stranger a lift from a truck stop. Not even an old man who says he just needs to get home.

There are good reasons for that rule. Insurance is one. If something happens with a stranger in your cab, you're on your own. But the real reason is that every driver who's been out here long enough has heard a story about a rider that ended badly. Some of those stories are true.

In ten years, I'd never once broken that rule. I was proud of it.

Here's the other thing you need to know. When you've got a weekend off in a big truck, you're not stuck. You can drop your trailer and drive the tractor alone, without the trailer, for your own reasons. Truckers call that bobtailing. And driving the truck off duty, for yourself, has a name too. Personal conveyance. Most companies let you do a little of it.

Not a five hundred mile round trip to the Canadian line with a stranger in the passenger seat.

I'm telling you all this so you understand that what I did that night was wrong. By every rule I had.

I did it anyway.

It was about four in the afternoon. I was in the truck stop restaurant with a cup of coffee, and an old man was going from booth to booth.

He was maybe seventy. Thin, straight-backed, in a heavy wool overcoat that was too warm for September, and a gray felt hat with a narrow brim, the kind men wore in old movies. In one hand he carried a little suitcase made of brown cardboard, tied shut with a piece of twine.

He'd stop at a booth, take off his hat, say something quiet, and the driver would shake his head. Every one of them. He'd thank them, put his hat back on, and move to the next booth.

When he got to me, he took off his hat and said, "Excuse me, son. I'm trying to get home. It's up on the Hi-Line, north of here. I can pay."

The Hi-Line is what folks in Montana call the far north of the state, along Highway Two. Wheat and cattle and wind, and towns so small the grain elevator is the tallest thing for fifty miles. From Billings, it's a good four hours.

I said what I always said. "I'm sorry, sir. I can't take riders."

He nodded like he'd expected it. He said, "I understand. Thank you anyway." And he started to put his hat back on.

And then Buddy did something I'd never seen him do.

I'd left him in the truck with the window cracked. The truck was parked right outside the restaurant window. And Buddy, who barked at every stranger who walked within fifty feet of that truck, was standing up on my passenger seat with his front paws on the dash, looking through two windows at that old man.

Not barking. Just looking. With his tail going.

The old man saw him too. He smiled, and his whole face changed. He said, "That's a good dog."

So let me ask you something.

You've got a rule. A good rule. You've kept it for ten years. And an old man with a cardboard suitcase is standing in front of you with his hat in his hand, and every other driver in the room has already said no.

And your dog, who doesn't trust anybody, is wagging his tail at him through the glass.

Do you break your rule?

I did. I don't fully know why. I think I was tired of being the kind of man who says no to an old man with his hat in his hand.

His name was Ellis. That's all he gave me. Just Ellis.

He climbed up into the cab slower than I expected, holding that suitcase in his lap like it was full of eggs. And Buddy, who never gave up his seat for anybody, hopped down into the sleeper without being asked, lay down right behind the old man, and put his chin on the edge of the bunk, looking at him.

We went north out of Billings with the sun going down on our left.

Ellis didn't talk much at first. When he did, it was about the country. He knew every creek we crossed by name. He knew which ranches had been homesteads and which had been bought up by somebody in town. Some of the things he told me sounded like they came out of a history book. He talked about a dance hall that I'm pretty sure burned down before I was born.

I offered him coffee from my thermos. He held the cup in both hands the whole way. He never drank from it.

And here's a thing I didn't think much about at the time. The farther north we went, the colder the cab got.

I had the heater going. It was September, and still warm outside. But by the time we hit the Hi-Line, I could see my breath. I thought the heater core was going out.

Buddy got up once, came forward, and pressed himself against my leg, shivering. Then he went back and lay down behind Ellis again. He never took his eyes off him.

About an hour from the end, Ellis started to talk.

He told me he'd grown up on a ranch up there. Just him and his father. His mother passed when he was little. His father was a hard man, he said. Not cruel. Just hard, the way that country makes you.

When Ellis was eighteen, they had a fight. The kind of fight where both people say the one thing they can't take back. His father wanted him to stay and take over the ranch. Ellis wanted to get out and see the country. He wanted to drive trucks.

"I walked out in a snowstorm," he said. "With this suitcase. I was going to show him."

He was quiet for a mile or two.

Then he said, "I've been trying to get home ever since."

I asked him how long ago that was. He didn't answer. He just pointed through the windshield and said, "Your turn's coming up. Gravel road on the right, past the grain elevator. Slow down for the cattle guard."

It was full dark when we turned off. The gravel road ran north for about eleven miles, straight as a ruler, with nothing on either side but wheat stubble and starlight. Then it ended at a gate.

The gate was rusted half off its hinges. Past it, up a little rise, there was a ranch house.

What was left of one.

Half the roof had fallen in. The porch sagged in the middle like a hammock. There was a windmill beside the house with half its blades gone, turning slow in the wind, and every time it came around it made one long creak, like a door opening in an empty house.

And there was a light in the kitchen window.

A soft yellow light, the kind a kerosene lamp makes. In a house with no roof, eleven miles from the nearest power line.

I stopped the truck. I didn't say anything. I didn't know what to say.

Ellis reached into his coat and took out a long envelope, thick, yellowed at the edges. He put it on the dash.

"That's for the ride," he said. "It was always meant for whoever brought me. Don't open it till I'm inside."

Then he put his hat on, picked up his suitcase, and climbed down out of my truck.

Buddy whined, once, and pushed his nose against the glass.

I watched Ellis walk through that broken gate and up the rise toward the house. He didn't use a flashlight. He walked like a man who knew where every rock was. He went up the porch steps, opened the door, and went inside, and the door closed behind him.

The light in the kitchen window stayed on for maybe a minute.

Then it went out.

Just the windmill after that. Creak. And the wind. Creak.

I sat there for twenty minutes. I told myself he was saying goodbye to the place. That he had people coming. That I'd wait until I saw a car.

No car came.

Buddy had stopped whining. He was lying on his blanket on the passenger seat, calm, looking at the house. Like whatever he'd been waiting for had happened.

I opened the envelope.

Inside was a stack of hundred dollar bills, wrapped in a paper bank band. Old bills, with small portraits, the kind you only see in a museum or a coin shop. The band was stamped with the name of a bank in a town I'd never heard of, and a date. February nineteen fifty-six.

I counted it twice, with the dome light on and my hands shaking. Ten thousand dollars.

And folded around the money was a check.

An old blue bank check, the paper soft as cloth. Pay to the order of the driver who brings my boy home. Ten thousand dollars. Signed, Thomas Varner. Dated February twentieth, nineteen fifty-six. And in the memo line, in the same careful handwriting, two words. For Ellis.

I got my flashlight, and Buddy and I went up to that house.

I called out. Nobody answered. The front door wasn't even latched. Half of it had rotted away at the bottom.

Inside, you could see the stars through the roof. There were dead leaves and old newspapers and the smell of a place that's been empty a very long time. The dust on the floor was thick as flour.

And here's the part I've thought about every day since.

The only footprints in that dust were mine. Mine, and Buddy's.

I went into the kitchen. There was a table, and two chairs. On the table was an old kerosene lamp. I touched the glass chimney. It was cold, and so full of dust I couldn't see the wick.

And on one of the chairs, there was a suitcase.

Brown cardboard. Tied shut with twine.

It was covered in the same thick gray dust as everything else in that house. Like it had been sitting on that chair for fifty years.

I didn't touch it. I'm not ashamed to tell you I backed out of that kitchen and walked to my truck without turning around, and Buddy walked right beside me the whole way, and I drove eleven miles of gravel faster than I've ever driven a gravel road.

There was a little sheriff's substation in the next town, closed for the night, with a phone on the outside wall. I called the number on the door. A deputy met me there at about one in the morning. Young guy, born and raised in that county.

I told him I'd dropped off an old man at the Varner place, and I couldn't find him.

He looked at me for a long time. Then he said, "What did he look like?"

I told him. The overcoat. The felt hat. The cardboard suitcase.

He took off his own hat and sat down on the curb.

Then he told me about Ellis Varner.

Every kid in that county grew up hearing about him. In February of nineteen fifty-six, Ellis Varner, eighteen years old, had a fight with his father and walked out to the highway in a snowstorm with a cardboard suitcase. A cattle truck going west picked him up. Somewhere out on the Hi-Line, in the storm, the truck went off the road into a coulee.

They didn't find them until the snow melted. The driver, and Ellis.

His suitcase was never found.

His father, Tom Varner, took ten thousand dollars out of the bank the week Ellis disappeared. Every dollar he had. He was going to pay pilots to search, and pay whoever found his boy. Nobody ever knew what happened to that money. Tom lived alone on that ranch for fifteen more years. Folks said he kept the porch light on every night. He died in nineteen seventy-one, and the place has been empty since.

Then the deputy said something I've never forgotten.

"My grandma knew Ellis," he said. "She said he was the best-looking boy in the county. Eighteen years old, forever."

And all I could think was, he wasn't eighteen. Not in my truck. He was seventy. The age he'd have been if he'd lived.

Wherever Ellis Varner had been all those years, he got old. Like he'd been on the road the whole time. Trying to get home.

I showed the deputy the check and the money. He read the check three times. Then he handed it back to me.

"Says right there who it belongs to," he said. "I'd say you're the driver."

I didn't keep the money. Not for me.

That fall, I went back up the Hi-Line with Buddy. Ellis Varner was buried in a little cemetery north of town, next to his father, under a wooden cross that had rotted down to a stump. I used part of that money to buy him a real stone. Granite. It says his name, the two years, and one word.

Home.

The rest went to the county's volunteer search and rescue. The folks who go out in snowstorms looking for people who didn't make it home. I figured Tom Varner would've liked that.

I kept the check. It's in my glove box tonight, about four feet from where I'm sitting. It's been in every truck I've driven since.

Buddy lay down by that grave for a long time, the day we set the stone. Then he got up, shook himself off, and trotted back to the truck like the job was done.

Here's what I think about, all these years later. A father wrote a check to a stranger he'd never meet, for everything he had, and waited fifteen years for somebody to cash it. And his boy spent fifty-two years trying to get it delivered.

Everybody out here is trying to get home. Some of us just take the long way.

Now, I want to say something serious, because some of you are going to hear this story and get the wrong idea.

Don't pick up strangers. I mean that. I broke that rule one time in thirty-one years, and it turned out to be the most important ride I ever gave. But that's not a reason for you to do it. If somebody at a truck stop needs help, you can help them without putting them in your cab. Call a trooper. Call a chaplain. Buy them a meal. Make sure somebody knows.

And if you've got a fight with somebody you love, and it's the kind where you both said the thing you can't take back, don't wait fifty years. Call them. Tonight, if you can.

Tomorrow morning, I'm going to drive up the Hi-Line. I do it every year about this time. I'll stop at that little cemetery and wipe off the stone. Buddy's not with me anymore. That's a story for another night. But I always leave a cup of coffee on the grass by the stone.

Ellis never did drink his.

Next time, a weigh station inspector who was writing tickets to truckers and putting the money in his own pocket. He picked the wrong driver. Actually, he picked about forty of the wrong drivers, all on the same night.

Before you go, let me ask you something. Have you ever met somebody who was just trying to get home? Tell me about it. I read every one.

Drive safe out there. And get home.
