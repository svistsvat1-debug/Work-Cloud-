That's the part I can't get past. Not the empty houses. Not the boarded windows. Not the sign at the edge of town that said nobody had lived there since nineteen fifty-one.

It was nineteen degrees that night. The coffee in my cup holder had a skin of ice on it. And on every clothesline in that town, there were sheets and shirts and long johns hanging in the moonlight, dripping. Dripping onto the frozen ground.

And on the last line, in front of the last house, there was a little girl's yellow dress.

It was missing a button. And somewhere behind me, in the dark, a woman started calling—

Hey. It's Hank.

Tonight I'm parked in Nevada, on Highway Fifty. They call it the Loneliest Road in America, and on a night like this, it earns the name. There's a truck stop behind me, and nothing in front of me for a hundred miles but sagebrush and stars.

Last time I told you about forty trucks and a crooked scale in New Mexico. A lot of you told me your own stories about people coming together. Keep them coming. I read every one.

Tonight is a strange one. I've only told it to one person before, and she was there before I was.

Pull up a chair. And if there's a dryer running somewhere in your house tonight, you might want to keep it running.

It was Saturday, November nineteenth, two thousand eleven. About eleven at night. I was thirty-four years old. Buddy had been riding with me for seven years by then, and he'd gone a little gray around the muzzle, same as me.

My father had been gone a year and a half. I mention that because I'd been thinking about him a lot that fall. More than I let on.

I had a load of mining equipment on a flatbed, headed west across Nevada on Highway Fifty, and about an hour past the last town, I came up on flashing lights. A wreck up ahead. A cattle truck on its side across both lanes, and a trooper waving everybody back. He said the road would be closed until morning. Maybe noon.

There was a detour. A county road that ran north through the mountains and came back down to the highway forty miles on. The trooper said it was paved most of the way.

Most of the way.

Now, here's something you need to understand about trucks and GPS. In two thousand eleven, a lot of drivers used the same little GPS units people used in their cars. And a car GPS doesn't know you're seventy feet long and forty tons. It doesn't know about low bridges, or weight limits, or roads that turn to dirt in the mountains. It just knows the shortest line between two points.

Every old-timer will tell you the same thing. Trust the big paper road atlas. Trust the truck route signs. Never trust a little box that's never driven a truck.

And there's one more thing you need to know. You can't turn a tractor-trailer around just anywhere. On a narrow road, at night, there's nowhere to go. You can't back up a mile in the dark on a mountain road with no shoulder. So once you're committed to a road in a big truck, you're committed. You keep going until you find a place wide enough to turn. A parking lot. A crossroads. A town square.

I knew all of that. I'd known it for thirteen years.

That night, I was tired. And when the county road split, and the pavement went one way and my little GPS said the other way was eleven miles shorter, I listened to the box.

The pavement ended about half a mile later.

Gravel. Then dirt. Then a road so narrow the sagebrush scraped both sides of my trailer. Climbing. And there was nowhere to turn around.

Buddy sat up on his blanket and looked at me. If a dog can say I told you so, he said it.

I drove that dirt road for nine miles, in first and second gear, with my stomach in a knot. And then the road came over a rise, and there was a town.

An old mining town, down in a little bowl in the hills. Maybe thirty houses. A main street. A church with no steeple. The bones of a mine up on the hillside behind it, with a wooden headframe standing over the shaft like a gallows.

And at the edge of town, a sign. Hand-painted, faded, leaning on one post.

Lodestar. Population, and then a number that had been painted over with one word.

None.

I didn't care. I was just glad to see a main street wide enough to turn a truck around. I eased down into that town at walking speed, with my headlights on, looking for room.

That's when I saw the first clothesline.

It was in the yard of the first house. Two T-shaped posts and three lines between them, the way they used to do it. And on the lines, there were sheets. White sheets, hanging still in the cold.

I remember thinking, somebody lives here. A caretaker, maybe. Some old prospector who never left.

Then I saw the next yard. And the next one.

Every house in that town had a clothesline. And every clothesline was full.

Bedsheets. Pillowcases. Men's work shirts, the blue cotton kind. Overalls. Long johns. Dish towels. Diapers. It was like the whole town had done its wash that day and hung it out at the same time.

I stopped the truck in the middle of the main street. I looked at the thermometer on my dash. Nineteen degrees.

Here's the thing. Wet laundry freezes. Hang a wet shirt outside at nineteen degrees and in an hour it's as stiff as a board. You could snap it in half. My grandmother used to bring in frozen sheets in the wintertime and stand them up in the kitchen like boards.

This laundry wasn't frozen.

It was soft. It was moving a little in the wind. And it was dripping. I could see the drops coming off the hems in my headlights, and where they hit the frozen ground, they'd made little dark holes in the frost.

I'm going to be honest with you. I sat there for a long time with both hands on the wheel.

Then I told myself what you'd tell yourself. There's somebody here. People live out in these old towns sometimes. Hermits. Artists. Folks who don't want to be found. It's a Saturday. They did their wash. There's a trick to it I don't know about. There's an explanation.

So I got my flashlight, and I climbed down to go knock on a door and ask somebody where I could turn my truck around.

Buddy wouldn't come. That dog jumped out of my truck at every stop for seven years. That night he backed into the sleeper and lay flat, and he wouldn't look at me.

The air smelled like soap. That's what I noticed first when I got down. Not dust, not sage. Laundry soap. And bleach. Like a laundromat on a summer day.

I walked up to the first house. The front door had a sheet of plywood nailed over it. Old plywood, gray and split. The nails were rusted down to nothing. The windows were boarded too. No light. No smoke from the chimney. Nobody had come through that door in a very long time.

I went to the next house. Same thing. And the next.

I shined my light on the ground around the clotheslines. It was all frost. Clean, unbroken frost. No footprints going to the lines. No footprints coming back. Nobody had walked out to those lines that day. Nobody had walked anywhere.

And under the lines, in every yard, there were laundry baskets. Wicker baskets. Rotted down to a few gray splinters in the dirt.

The baskets were sixty years old. The laundry was still wet.

I'd seen enough. I walked fast back toward my truck, down the middle of the main street, with my light going from yard to yard. And that's when I got to the last house, the one at the end of the street, right before the road climbed up toward the mine.

That clothesline was different. It only had a few things on it. Three men's work shirts. A woman's apron. A pair of little girl's socks.

And a yellow dress.

A little girl's dress, about the size a six-year-old would wear, with a white collar. It was hanging by two wooden clothespins at the shoulders, and it was dripping like all the rest. And when my light hit it, I saw that the top button was missing. Just a few loose threads, where it used to be.

I don't know why that detail got me. It did. I stood there in the cold and looked at that little dress, and I thought about my own boy, who was thirteen that year and hadn't worn anything that small in a long time.

Then the wind stopped.

All at once, the way it does in the desert. And in that quiet, I heard a sound I knew from when I was a kid.

A screen door. Somewhere behind me, up the street, a screen door swung open on its spring and slapped shut.

And then a woman's voice. Not close. Not far. Clear as a bell, the way a mother's voice carries across a whole neighborhood at the end of the day.

"Ruthie! Supper!"

I didn't turn around. I'm not ashamed of that.

It came again. "Ruthie! Supper's on!"

And then nothing. The wind came back. The clotheslines started creaking again, all over town.

I walked to my truck without running, because I didn't want to know what would happen if I ran. I got in. Buddy was pressed so hard against the back wall of the sleeper, he was shaking the bunk. I swung that truck around in the main street in one move, a turn I'd have told you was impossible, and I drove nine miles of dirt road back to the pavement in the dark and didn't once look in my mirrors.

I sat at the bottom of that county road until the sun came up. Then I went back to Highway Fifty, and when they opened it, I drove forty miles to the next town and pulled into the first café I saw. I needed coffee, and I needed people.

The café had old photographs all over the walls. Mining days. Men in hard hats. Mule teams. And by the register, there was a photograph that made me put my cup down.

It was a picture of a town in a little bowl in the hills, with a mine headframe on the hillside behind it. And in every yard, there was laundry on the lines.

Under it, somebody had typed a little card. Lodestar, Nevada. Wash day. November nineteenth, nineteen fifty-one. The morning of the flood.

I asked the waitress about it. She didn't answer me. She just went down to the end of the counter, where an old woman was sitting by herself with a cup of tea, and said something to her quietly.

The old woman looked at me for a long time. Then she picked up her tea and came and sat down across from me.

Her name was Ruth. She was sixty-six. She'd been born in Lodestar.

Here's what she told me.

In nineteen fifty-one, Lodestar was a silver town. Maybe a hundred and twenty people. Monday was wash day. Every woman in town did the wash on Monday and hung it out on the lines, and it was a point of pride whose sheets were whitest.

On Monday, November nineteenth, at about two in the afternoon, the miners on the lower levels broke into an old flooded working. The water came through all at once. There were twenty-three men down there.

None of them came up.

Ruth's father was one of them. Joe. He was a shift boss. She said he had big hands and he whistled through his teeth.

The women and children stood at the mine head all that night, and the next day, and the day after that. And nobody went home to take in the wash. Four days later, the company closed the mine. Within a month, there was nobody left in Lodestar.

Ruth said her mother never went back for the laundry. A neighbor offered to fetch it. Her mother said, "Leave it. Those are his shirts. Let them wait for him."

Every family in town did the same. That's why there were clotheslines in a town where nobody lived. For sixty years, the wash just hung there and rotted away, and the wind took it a little at a time.

I didn't know how to tell her. I tried. I told her about the wet laundry, and the cold, and the frost with no footprints. She listened without saying a word.

Then I told her about the last house. The work shirts, and the apron, and the little socks. And the yellow dress.

She put down her tea.

She said, "Was it missing a button?"

I said, "The top one."

She opened her purse, and she took out a little coin purse, and out of that, she took a button. A small yellow button, the color of butter, worn smooth.

"It came off that morning," she said. "When I was getting dressed for school. Mama put it in her apron pocket and said she'd sew it back on after supper."

Then she asked me one more thing. She asked me if I'd heard anything.

I told her.

She sat very still. Then she said, "Every evening at dark, Mama would come out on the porch and call me in. The whole street could hear her. Ruthie, supper." She looked out the café window for a while. "That night, we were at the mine. Nobody called anybody in for supper. Not that night or any night after."

Then she said, "Do you know what day it was yesterday, young man?"

I didn't. I hadn't put it together.

It was November nineteenth. Sixty years to the day.

Ruth reached across the table and put her hand on mine. It was a small hand, and very cold. She said, "Thank you for telling me. I always wondered if she was still waiting."

I don't have an explanation for any of it. I've had fifteen years, and I don't have one. I know what I saw. I know what I heard. I know an old woman had a yellow button in her purse that matched a dress I'd seen hanging on a line twelve hours before.

Here's what stays with me. A whole town of women hung out the wash on a Monday morning because they expected everybody to come home for supper. That's all wash day is, when you think about it. It's a promise that life is going to go on the way it's supposed to.

And sometimes it doesn't.

Since that night, I call home every evening before dark. Wherever I am. Even if it's just to say I'm fine and the dog's fine and I'll call tomorrow. My wife used to tease me about it. She doesn't anymore.

If you drive anything big, a truck or a motor home or a pickup with a trailer, don't trust a car GPS. Carry a real road atlas. And if a road turns to dirt at night, stop right there and wait for daylight. A dirt road in the dark will always take you somewhere you can't turn around.

And if there's somebody waiting on you tonight, call them before dark. Just to say you're coming home.

Tomorrow I'll drive past that county road again, the one that runs north into the mountains. I won't take it. That town isn't mine to visit. But every Christmas, I get a card from Ruth. She's eighty-one now. And every year, the card says the same thing, in a shaky old hand.

Call home before dark.

Next time, it's the summer of two thousand twelve. My boy was fourteen, and he hadn't said ten words to me in a year. His mother put him in my truck for a week, and we drove across the country in total silence. Six days of nothing. And then one night, somewhere out west, something happened that changed both of us.

Before you go, let me ask you something. Who called you in for supper when you were a kid? Tell me about them. I read every one.

Drive safe out there. And call home before dark.
