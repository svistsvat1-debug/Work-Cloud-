Have you ever been one phone call away from walking out on your whole life?

I was. Two in the morning, at a truck stop at the bottom of a mountain in Tennessee. I'd written my resignation on a paper napkin. Four words. Marlene. I quit. Hank.

I had a pocket full of quarters, and I called the one man I was sure would be ashamed of me.

My father listened to the whole thing without saying a word.

Then he said, "Son. Before you do anything, I want you to—"

Hey. It's Hank.

Tonight I'm parked outside Laramie, Wyoming. I made it across. And yes, I flashed my lights at that dip by Elk Mountain, the way I told you I would. Nothing blinked back. I'll admit I was a little relieved.

Last time was a hard one. Tonight's a Sunday story. No blizzard, no strange headlights. Just a pay phone, a mountain, and my father.

Pull up a chair.

It was Tuesday, April twenty-second, two thousand three. I was twenty-six years old, five years into long haul, and I hadn't been home in thirty-six days.

I had forty-three thousand pounds of roofing shingles behind me, Nashville to Atlanta, on Interstate twenty-four. Due at eight in the morning.

Three days before, on Saturday, my boy turned five. I missed it. A load in Texas went bad, the receiver wouldn't unload me, and by the time they did, the party was over. I called the house that afternoon from a truck stop in Texarkana. My wife held the phone out to him, and I heard him in the background, with all his little friends yelling.

He said, "Mommy, it's the truck."

Not Daddy. The truck.

He didn't mean anything by it. He was five. To him, I was a voice that came out of the phone on Sundays and a big noise in the driveway every few weeks. But I sat there in that truck stop with the phone in my hand, and something in me cracked right down the middle.

My father drove a truck my whole life. A green Kenworth. And I couldn't remember him ever missing my birthday. Not once. I couldn't figure out how he did it, and I couldn't do it.

Now, to understand what happened that night, you need to know about a mountain.

Between Nashville and Chattanooga, Interstate twenty-four goes over Monteagle Mountain. If you drive a car, you've probably never noticed it. If you drive a truck, you know it by name. It's one of the most famous grades in the country, and not in a good way.

Here's the thing about a loaded truck going down a mountain. Your brakes are not enough. They're drums and shoes, and every time you use them, they get hotter. Get them hot enough, and they fade. The pedal goes soft, the smell comes up through the floor, like burnt toast and hot pennies, and the truck just keeps going.

So you don't use your brakes to go down a mountain. You use your engine. You pick a low gear at the top, and you turn on your engine brake. Truckers call it a Jake. It's that loud rattle you hear from big trucks coming down a hill, like a machine gun. The engine holds you back, and the brakes are just for help.

And there's one rule every driver learns his first year. You pick your gear at the top. Because if you try to shift on the way down and you miss it, even for a second, you're in neutral. No engine. Forty tons, rolling downhill, with nothing holding it but brakes that are already hot.

That's why mountains have runaway ramps. Long lanes of deep, loose gravel, running off the side of the road and uphill. If your brakes are gone, you steer into the gravel, and it grabs your wheels and buries you to the axles. It's ugly, it's expensive, and you live.

There's an old saying. You can go down a mountain too slow a thousand times. You can only go down too fast once.

On the way down Monteagle, there's a sign. Truckers, you are not down yet.

I passed it that night at about eleven o'clock. And I was going too fast.

I was late. I'd been late all week. I was tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix. And I did the thing every old-timer warns you about. I went over the top in too high a gear, telling myself I'd catch it on the way down.

Halfway down, I knew I was in trouble.

The truck was gaining. Fifty. Fifty-five. I had the Jake on, and it was roaring, and it wasn't enough. I touched the brakes, and I smelled them right away. Burnt toast. Hot pennies.

I had to get a lower gear. And there's no way to do that on a hill without going through neutral.

So I pushed in the clutch, and I pulled the stick out of gear, and for one second, the truck was free.

You can feel it. Forty-three thousand pounds of shingles, and nothing holding them. Just the hill, and the dark, and the sound of the tires.

I went for the lower gear, and I missed it.

The stick ground against the gears and bounced back at me. The speedometer climbed past sixty. And I'll tell you exactly what I thought about, because it surprised me. I didn't think about dying. I thought, my boy is going to remember me as the truck.

I tried again. I matched the engine to the road the way my father taught me when I was sixteen, in a hay field, in his green Kenworth. And the gear went in. The engine caught the truck like a hand catching a ball, and the Jake started hammering, and the needle started coming down.

Fifty-five. Fifty. Forty-five.

And then my headlights swept across the runaway ramp on the right.

There was already a truck in it.

A big red tractor with a flatbed, buried up to its fuel tanks in gravel, its four-ways blinking. The driver was standing next to it in the cold, smoking a cigarette, and his hand was shaking so bad I could see it from the road.

That was going to be me. One more missed gear, and that was me.

I made it to the bottom at thirty miles an hour, with my brakes smoking, and I pulled into the first truck stop I saw, and I parked in the back row, and I sat there with both hands on the wheel for a long time.

Then I went inside.

It was almost two in the morning. A waitress was mopping the floor under the stools. I sat in a booth, and I ordered coffee I didn't drink, and I pulled a napkin out of the holder and I wrote on it.

Marlene. I quit. Hank.

Marlene was my dispatcher back then. She'd been dispatching trucks since before I was born, and she called every driver hon. I'd tell her in the morning. I'd deliver the shingles, and I'd quit, and I'd find a job in Ohio where I came home every night and nobody called me the truck.

I had it all figured out.

My cell phone was dead. I'd left the charger in a motel in Texas. So I went to the pay phone by the restrooms with a pocket full of quarters.

I didn't call my wife. I want to be honest with you about why. If I called her, she'd say come home. And I wanted somebody to tell me I was wrong. Or right. I didn't know which.

So I called my father.

He picked up on the first ring. At two in the morning. He said, "Hank."

Not hello. Just my name. Like he'd been expecting me.

And I told him everything. The birthday. The truck. The mountain. The missed gear. The ramp, and the man with the shaking hand. And the napkin.

He didn't say a word the whole time. I could hear the clock in my mother's kitchen ticking behind him.

When I was done, I waited for it. My father had never been late a day in his life. I waited for him to tell me I was soft, or that I'd made my bed. I'd have taken it.

Instead, he said, "Okay."

That was all, for a while. Okay.

Then he said, "Son. Before you do anything, I want you to listen to me. Two in the morning lies. You hear me? Two in the morning lies, and so does the bottom of a mountain. Nobody ever made a good decision at two in the morning. Go out to your truck and go to sleep. If you still want to quit when the sun's up, I'll come get you myself."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. He was four hundred miles away, in Ohio.

I said, "Dad, you can't come get me."

And he said, "Go to sleep, Hank."

And he hung up.

So let me ask you something. And I want you to really think about it before you answer.

If it was two in the morning, and you were at the bottom of your own mountain, who would you call? Not who you'd text. Who would you actually call, knowing they'd pick up on the first ring?

Some of you have an answer right away. Some of you don't. I've been both.

I went out to my truck. I lay down in the bunk with my boots on. I thought I'd never sleep. I was gone in about a minute.

When I woke up, the sun was coming through the curtain, and it was a quarter after nine.

And there was a sound outside I'd known my whole life. A big diesel, idling, with a little lope in it, like it was humming to itself.

I pulled back the curtain.

Parked right next to my truck, close enough to touch, was a green Kenworth.

My father was sitting in the driver's seat with his cap pulled down over his eyes and his arms folded, asleep. On his dash were two cups of coffee from the truck stop. One for him. One for me. Both of them cold.

He'd hung up the phone at two in the morning, put on his boots, and driven four hundred miles through the dark. To a truck stop he'd never seen. Because he'd told me he would.

I knocked on his door. He opened one eye, looked at me, and said, "Sun's up. You still want to quit?"

I couldn't answer him. I'd like to tell you I said something wise. I didn't say anything at all.

We had breakfast in that same booth. Eggs, sausage, biscuits. He ate like a man who'd been driving all night, which he had. And when he was done, he wiped his mouth, and he told me something I'd never heard in twenty-six years.

In nineteen seventy-nine, the year he bought that green Kenworth, he almost quit too.

I was two. The truck payments were eating him alive. He was gone three weeks at a time. And one night, he said, at a truck stop in Arkansas, he wrote it out on the back of a fuel receipt and decided he was done.

"I quit about once a month for three years," he said. "Always at two in the morning. Never once at noon."

Then he reached up to his sun visor and pulled down a picture I had never seen.

It was me. Two years old. In a diaper and a cowboy hat, sitting on the front bumper of a brand new green Kenworth, crying my eyes out because I didn't want to get down.

"That's been up there twenty-four years," he said.

I didn't know what to say. I had a picture of my own boy tucked in my own visor, in the exact same spot. I'd never once thought about where I got the idea.

Then my father said the thing I've carried ever since.

"I didn't drive down here to talk you out of anything, son. You want to quit, quit. You'll be a good daddy either way. I came down here so you wouldn't have to decide by yourself."

I picked up the napkin. It was still on the table. Marlene. I quit. Hank.

He took it out of my hand, turned it over, and wrote on the back with the pen from the check holder. Then he slid it back to me.

It said, Not on a mountain. Not at two in the morning. Dad.

Then he finished his coffee, paid for both of us, and drove four hundred miles home. He had a load out of Columbus that night.

I delivered my shingles in Atlanta. Then I called Marlene and told her I needed a week at home.

She was quiet for a second. Then she said, "Hon, I've been waiting two years for you to ask me that."

When I pulled into my driveway, my boy hid behind his mother's legs for about a minute, like I was somebody new. Then I took off my old cap and put it on his head. It came down over his eyes. He wore it for the rest of the week. He wore it to bed. The next year, he wore it for Halloween.

After that week, I went back out. Nobody talked me into it. I decided it at noon, on my own front porch, with the sun up. And I didn't miss his next birthday.

That napkin is still taped to the inside of my sun visor, right next to his picture. It's been through four trucks.

Now, there's one more thing. And I didn't find it out until a long time later.

My father passed in March of two thousand ten. He was fifty-nine. After the funeral, I sat in my mother's kitchen, at that same table, and I told her about the night on Monteagle. I figured she knew. She didn't. He'd never told her a word of it.

But she remembered that night.

She said she woke up around one-thirty in the morning, and his side of the bed was empty. She found him sitting at the kitchen table, dressed, with his boots on and his keys in his hand. Just sitting there, in the dark, looking at the phone.

She asked him what in the world he was doing.

And he said, "Our boy's on a mountain."

She said the phone rang about half an hour later.

I've thought about that a lot. I never told him which mountain I was going to cross that night. I hadn't talked to him in two weeks. I don't know how he knew, and my mother doesn't either. Maybe a father just knows. I'd like to think so.

Here's what I know for sure. My father didn't save my job that night. He never even tried. He showed me what you do when somebody you love is at the bottom of a mountain at two in the morning.

You go.

Last spring, my phone rang at two in the morning. It was my boy. He's twenty-eight now, and what he was going through is his story, not mine. He said, "Dad, I think I'm done."

I said, "Son. Two in the morning lies. Go to sleep. If you still feel this way when the sun's up, I'll come get you myself."

I was eight hundred miles away. I dropped my trailer and I drove all night.

By the time I got there, he'd already figured it out on his own. That's all right. That was never the point.

If you take one thing from tonight, take this. If you ever drive a mountain in anything heavy, a truck, a motor home, a pickup with a trailer, pick your gear at the top and go down slower than you came up. If you smell your brakes, it's already late. Pull over where it's safe and let them cool. And if you see a runaway ramp and you need it, use it. There's no shame in gravel.

And whatever mountain you're on, don't decide anything at two in the morning.

Next time, we're going to Montana. An old man at a truck stop asked me for a ride to the house he grew up in. When we got there, he handed me an envelope with ten thousand dollars in it. And a check dated nineteen fifty-six.

Before you go, let me ask you something. Who showed up for you when you were ready to quit? Tell me about them. I read every one.

Drive safe out there. And don't quit at two in the morning.
