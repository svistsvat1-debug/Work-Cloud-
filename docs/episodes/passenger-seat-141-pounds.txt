I was driving alone. Nobody in the passenger seat. Nobody in the sleeper.

But for five hundred and forty miles, my truck weighed one hundred and forty-one pounds too much. Three scales in a row. And every one of those pounds was sitting on the front axle. Right under the passenger seat.

Then the sun came up, and I pulled down the visor. An old scale ticket fell into my lap.

Somebody had written two weights on it in pencil. The difference was one hundred and forty-one pounds.

And next to the bigger number, a woman's name…

Hey. It's Hank.

Tonight I'm parked at a truck stop outside Amarillo, Texas. The wind out here is blowing like it's late for something. And the coffee tastes like the pot has been on since Tuesday. I'm drinking it anyway.

Tonight's story is about a passenger. A passenger who rode with me across three states, and I never saw her once. The only one who knew she was there was the scale.

It was Thursday, September thirteenth, twenty twelve. I was thirty-five years old.

My own truck was in the shop that week. Bad injector, and the part was on back order. So the company gave me a loaner out of the back row of the yard. Number four oh nine. An older long-nose truck with a big sleeper and a lot of miles on it.

It was clean inside. Almost too clean. Like nobody had really lived in it for a while.

I picked up a load of newsprint at a paper mill in Longview, Washington. Big rolls of paper, the kind they print newspapers on. One roll weighs more than a small car. I was taking them to a printing plant in Salt Lake City.

Interstate Five south to Portland. Then Interstate Eighty-Four east, up the Columbia River, across Oregon, across Idaho, and down into Utah. About eight hundred and twenty miles. Two easy days.

At least that's what I thought.

Now, if you've never driven a truck, let me tell you something about weight.

A loaded truck is allowed to weigh eighty thousand pounds. But the total isn't the only thing that matters. That weight has to be spread out right, across the axles.

You've got three groups. The steer axle up front, under the engine and the cab. The drive axles under the back of the truck. And the trailer axles, way at the back.

Every group has a limit. And the steer axle is the touchy one. Put too much weight up front, and the truck gets heavy in your hands. Harder to turn. Harder on the front tires. Our company had a rule. Keep the steer axle under twelve thousand pounds. No exceptions.

So after you load, you go to a truck stop with a certified scale. You pull onto a long steel platform, one axle group on each section. You push a button and talk to somebody inside. A minute later, they hand you a ticket with three numbers on it.

Here's the part most people don't know. The driver shows up on that ticket too. You sit right over the steer axle. So does your passenger. If your wife rides along with you, she's in that steer axle number. Some drivers used to tease their wives about it.

And one more thing. Diesel weighs about seven pounds a gallon. Every mile you drive, you burn some, and the truck gets a little lighter. I keep a fuel log. I always have. So I always know about how much fuel I've burned, and about how much lighter I should be.

Remember that. A truck only gets lighter as it goes. Never heavier.

I weighed at a truck stop right across the road from the mill. It was about noon. Steer axle, eleven thousand eight hundred and twenty pounds. Under the line, with room to spare. Good.

I parked at the edge of the lot to do my paperwork. Across the street there was an old motel. Two stories, doors facing the parking lot, and a sign that said weekly rates.

I was writing in my logbook when I heard it.

A long, soft hiss. Right next to me.

Trucks like that have air-ride seats. There's a little air bag under the cushion, and it holds you up like a pillow. When somebody sits down, the air pushes out with a sigh.

I looked over. The passenger seat had sunk all the way to the bottom. Like somebody had just sat down in it.

There was nobody there, of course. I figured the air bag had a leak. Old truck, old seat. I pushed on the cushion. It was low and soft, and it stayed that way. I made a note to tell the shop.

And I pulled out onto the interstate.

By the time I got into the Columbia River Gorge, I could feel that something was off. The steering was heavy. Not a lot. Just enough. Like the truck was leaning on my arms a little.

I've driven a lot of trucks. You learn how they feel, the way you learn a horse. And this one felt loaded up front.

There's a truck stop in the Gorge with a scale. I pulled in and weighed again. Paid my few dollars and took my ticket.

Steer axle, eleven thousand nine hundred and forty-one.

I sat there and did the math twice. I'd burned close to twenty-five gallons since Longview. By my fuel log, that steer axle should have been about eleven thousand eight hundred. It was one hundred and forty-one pounds over that.

Nothing else had changed. The drive axles were right. The trailer was right. All of the extra weight was in one place. Up front, under the cab.

I went through that truck with a flashlight. The sleeper. The cabinets. Under the bunk. The side boxes outside. The battery box. The catwalk behind the cab. I even lay down on the gravel and looked up under the bumper.

Nothing. Not a toolbox, not a jug of water, not a stray cat.

And the passenger seat was still sitting on the bottom.

I drove on. Up out of the Gorge, into the dry country of eastern Oregon. The wheat stubble there goes gold all the way to the sky. I kept both hands on the wheel. The truck kept leaning on me.

Near the Idaho line it was getting dark. I stopped for the night at a big truck stop. And before I parked, I weighed one more time.

Steer axle, one hundred and forty-one pounds heavy. Again. After four hundred miles of fuel.

The same number. To the pound.

Now, a scale can be off. One scale. Maybe two. But three different scales in three different towns don't make the same mistake to the exact pound.

So what was riding up front with me? And why did it weigh exactly one hundred and forty-one pounds?

I didn't sleep much that night.

I lay in the bunk and looked at the passenger seat through the gap in the curtain. In the dark, it looked like somebody was sitting there. Looking out at the parking lot.

You know, in most trucks, the passenger seat is the loneliest seat in America. It's where you throw your jacket. Your lunch bag. Your paperwork. Hardly anybody ever sits there.

I've had a wife waiting at home my whole career. And I've spent most of it talking to an empty seat. About the weather. About the traffic. About my day. You do that long enough, and you stop noticing that nobody answers.

That night, for the first time in years, I had the feeling that somebody was listening.

I left at first light, heading east. The sun came up over the hills and right into my eyes.

So I reached up and pulled down the visor. Something slid out from behind it and dropped into my lap.

It was a scale ticket. Old and soft, the ink faded almost to nothing. The date on it said April twenty-second, two thousand nine. Same truck number. Four oh nine.

Somebody had written on the back in pencil. I pulled onto the shoulder to read it.

The first line said, "With June. Eleven thousand four hundred and one."

The second line said, "Without June. Eleven thousand two hundred and sixty."

And under that it said, "She says the scale is lying. Love you anyway. D."

I did the math right there on the shoulder, with trucks blowing past and rocking my cab.

One hundred and forty-one pounds.

I sat there a long time. Then I looked over at the passenger seat. Still on the bottom.

"Good morning, June," I said. I don't know why. It just felt rude not to.

At seven o'clock I called the shop. The foreman picked up. A man named Ruben, who had been with that company longer than the paint on the building.

I asked him who used to drive four oh nine.

He was quiet for a second.

"Dale Harmon," he said. "Dale and June. They ran that truck together for years. She didn't drive. She rode with him. Did the maps, did the crosswords, kept him awake."

Then he told me the rest.

In the spring of twenty ten, they were waiting on a load in Longview. June wasn't feeling good, so Dale got her a room at a little motel across from the truck stop. She lay down to rest while he went to get loaded.

Her heart stopped while he was gone. She was sixty-one.

"They were two weeks from retiring," Ruben said. "Dale drove that truck back here empty. He parked it in the back row, gave me the keys, and never drove again."

"Where did he go?" I asked.

"Home to Idaho," he said. "Little place called Juniper Flat. East of Boise."

I asked him one more thing. I asked if four oh nine had been back to Longview since then.

I heard him typing.

"No," he said. "That's the first load she's pulled out of there since Dale. Why? Something wrong with her?"

I looked at the passenger seat.

"No," I said. "She's riding fine."

So let me ask you something.

A truck that sat in the back row for two years. A woman who died in a motel across the street from that truck stop in Longview. The first time that truck ever went back, the passenger seat sighed and sat down. Three scales in three towns said the same thing. One hundred and forty-one pounds. The same number a husband once wrote on a ticket, just to tease his wife.

And now that truck was carrying her east. Toward Idaho. Toward home.

You tell me.

Who was riding in my passenger seat?

I went through Boise in the middle of the morning. Then out into the high desert, with sagebrush and black lava rock on both sides.

About sixty miles past Boise, I saw the sign. Juniper Flat, next exit.

And as I came up on that exit, the steering went light in my hands. All at once. Like somebody had let go of my arm.

I heard a soft hiss. I looked over. The passenger seat was rising back up. All the way up. Like somebody had just stood.

I took the exit. I don't think I could have driven past it.

Juniper Flat is a gas station, a church, a grain elevator, and maybe forty houses. I asked for Dale Harmon at the gas station. The lady behind the counter pointed up the road.

A small white house. Tomato plants along the fence. And an old man on the porch, reading the newspaper.

I told him who I was. I told him I was driving four oh nine. Then I handed him the ticket.

He knew it right away. He held it in both hands and laughed. And then he wasn't laughing anymore.

"She hated that ticket," he said. "Hid it from me three times. I always found it."

I told him everything. The seat in Longview. The three scales. The exit.

I expected him to think I was crazy. He didn't even look surprised.

"She always said it," Dale said. "If I go first, I'll ride the last load home. And I left her there. I drove home with that seat empty, and I left her in Longview."

He looked up the hill. There was a little cemetery up there with a white fence around it, looking down at the interstate.

"You can see the exit from her grave," he said. "I picked the spot so she could watch the trucks."

We sat on his porch for an hour. He made me a sandwich. He told me June used to call herself the ballast. She said that was her job. To keep him on the road.

When I left, he walked me out to the truck. He put his hand flat on the passenger door and kept it there a while. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

At the next scale, my steer axle was exactly what it should have been. To the pound.

There's one more thing. And I've never been able to explain it.

I brought four oh nine back to the yard. I told Ruben the passenger seat needed a new air bag. That it had been sitting on the bottom since Longview.

He climbed in and looked under it. Then he climbed back out and gave me a funny look.

"Hank," he said. "That seat doesn't have any air. Dale had me cap the line after June died. He didn't want anybody sitting in her seat."

That seat had no air in it for two years. It couldn't sigh for anybody.

But I heard it sigh. In Longview. And I heard it again at that exit.

So here's what I'll leave you with.

If somebody has been asking to ride along with you, take them. On a run, on a trip, to the hardware store. Whatever your road is. Don't wait for retirement. Dale and June were two weeks short.

The seat next to you won't always have somebody in it. Fill it while you can.

Next time, I've got another one from the road.

And now I want to hear from you. Who rode in your passenger seat? A wife, a buddy, your dad, a dog? Tell me about them in the comments. I read every one.

Drive safe out there.

And if your truck ever weighs a little heavy, don't be so quick to blame the scale. Maybe somebody just needs a ride home.
