That house has had no electricity since nineteen ninety-four. The power line is lying in the weeds. I've seen it with my own eyes.

But for two years, every time my truck came over that hill at night, the porch light came on. Two hundred and six times. I counted.

Then one Friday in April, at ten after one in the morning, I came over that hill. And the porch stayed dark.

And down in the hollow, below the old road, something flashed at me through the trees.

Two short flashes. One long. Then it started again…

Hey. It's Hank.

Tonight I'm parked at a truck stop in northern Michigan. The first snow of the year is coming sideways off the lake. And the coffee in this place could pull a trailer by itself. I'm not complaining.

Tonight I want to tell you about a porch light. A porch light on a house with no power, that only came on for trucks. And about the one night it didn't.

It was April of twenty sixteen. A Friday. I was thirty-nine years old, and for almost two years I'd been running the same dedicated route every week. Auto parts out of Ohio, south through the mountains of West Virginia, down into North Carolina. Tuesday nights going down. Friday nights coming back up. Same road, same stops, same coffee.

A dedicated route is a strange thing. After a few months, you know every bump. Every billboard. Every dog that barks at the same gas station. You stop seeing the road, and you start seeing the little things beside it.

That's how I noticed the house.

It sat on a knob above the highway, at the top of a long grade. An old two-story farmhouse with a wide porch. No other houses for miles. And every time I came over that hill at night, the porch light came on. Just for a few seconds. Then it went dark again.

I keep a fuel log. Always have. Every gallon, every stop, every price, written down in a little spiral notebook. After the first month on that route, I started putting a small mark in the back of that notebook. One mark every time the porch light came on. Don't ask me why. A man alone in a truck all night starts counting things. Mile markers. Water towers. Porch lights.

Now, if you never drove a truck in the mountains, let me tell you what that hill meant.

A loaded truck can weigh eighty thousand pounds. Going up a mountain is slow, but it's safe. Going down is where people get hurt. If you ride your brakes all the way down a long grade, they get hot. And hot brakes stop working. We call it brake fade. You can smell it before you feel it. A sharp, burnt smell, like a pan left on the stove.

So the old rule is simple. You go down a hill in the same gear you came up it. You let the engine hold you back, not the brakes. And on the steep ones, the state builds runaway truck ramps. A long lane of deep gravel running uphill off the side of the road. If your brakes are gone, you aim for the gravel and you pray.

That hill below the farmhouse was a seven percent grade, four miles long, with a hard curve at the bottom. The highway there is a nice four-lane now. But back in the eighties it was a narrow two-lane road that hugged the side of the mountain. In nineteen eighty-seven they cut the new road straight through the rock and left the old one to the woods. You can still see a piece of it from the four-lane if you know where to look. An old guardrail. A curve of cracked blacktop, going nowhere.

Keep that old road in your mind. It matters.

Every driver on that run did the same thing at the top of that hill. We dropped a gear, we checked our mirrors, and we looked up at the farmhouse. And when the porch light came on, a lot of us flashed our headlights back. Nobody ever talked about why. It was just something you did.

I did it for months without thinking about it. A light comes on, you flash back. Like waving at a neighbor.

Then one morning in March, I came up that hill in daylight for the first time. A load had been late, and my whole week was running backwards. And in daylight, I saw the house for what it was.

The windows were broken. The porch roof sagged in the middle like an old horse. There was a tree growing up through the front steps. And the power pole by the road had snapped off years ago. The wire was lying in the weeds, rusted, coiled like a dead snake.

Nobody lived there. Nobody had lived there for a long time.

I pulled into the diner at the bottom of the grade. A little place with six stools and a pie case. The waitress there was named Bonnie, and she'd poured my coffee maybe a hundred times. I asked her about the farmhouse on the hill.

She stopped wiping the counter.

"That's the Ferris place," she said. "Nobody's lived there since Ruth died. That was nineteen ninety-four."

"Somebody wired the porch light," I said. "It comes on at night. When trucks come over the hill."

Bonnie looked at me for a long time.

"Honey," she said, "it doesn't come on for cars. Have you noticed that? Only for trucks."

I hadn't noticed. But she was right. I'd driven that road in my pickup once, on a weekend. The house stayed dark the whole way past.

Then she told me the story.

Ruth Ferris had one son. Danny. He drove a truck, like his daddy before him. And every night Danny was on the road, Ruth left that porch light on. When Danny came over the top of the hill, he'd see the light. And he'd flash his headlights at the house. Two short, one long. That was his signal. It meant, I'm close, Mama. Put the coffee on.

On a Saturday night in January, nineteen eighty-five, there was snow on the mountain. Danny called his mother from a truck stop sixty miles south. He said he'd be home by one. Ruth turned the porch light on and sat down by the window.

Danny never came over the hill.

They searched for weeks. The state police, the county, every driver on that road. They never found him. Not him. Not his truck. Not a scratch on a guardrail. Twenty-four years old, and he just vanished off the side of a mountain.

Ruth left the porch light on every night for nine years. Until the night she died.

"Then the power company cut the line," Bonnie said. "And about a year after that, the drivers started talking. Saying the light still comes on."

She poured me another cup I didn't ask for.

"Every truck," she said. "Every night. Like she's still checking to see if it's him."

I didn't believe it. Not completely. So the next Tuesday, I came over the hill just before dark. I pulled onto the shoulder below the house. I climbed up through the wet grass with a flashlight and stood on that rotten porch.

The fixture by the door was an old glass dome, full of dead moths. The bulb inside was black with dust. I looked through the broken window next to the door. On the wall inside, there was a light switch.

It was in the on position.

Ruth had left it on. And nobody, in twenty-two years, had ever turned it off.

So how does a light with no wire come on two hundred times? And who, or what, was still watching that road?

I drove north that night thinking about Ruth Ferris sitting by a window for nine years.

Here's something I don't say much. My mother did the same thing. When I started driving, she'd leave the porch light on whenever she knew I was headed home. Even if I wasn't getting in till four in the morning. She still does it. She's seventy-four years old, and if she knows I'm coming, that light is on.

When you drive for a living, your family spends a lot of nights looking at a dark road. You don't think about it much when you're young. You think about the next load, the next check, the next cup of coffee. Then one day you're almost forty, and you realize somebody has been waiting up for you your whole life.

I called my mother from the next truck stop. It was three in the morning. She picked up on the second ring.

For the next month, I watched that house like a hawk.

The light came on every single time. Tuesday nights going down. Friday nights coming back. Rain or fog, it didn't matter. I'd come over the top of the grade, and that old porch would glow yellow for about four seconds. I started flashing back the way Danny did. Two short, one long. I don't know why. It just felt like the right thing to do.

Then came that Friday in April.

It was the twenty-second. I came up out of North Carolina with a trailer full of empty parts racks. Light as a feather. Running about forty minutes late. It had rained all evening, and the fog was sitting down in the hollows like milk in a bowl. I came over the top of the hill at ten after one in the morning.

And the porch stayed dark.

I actually slowed down. I looked up at the house, and it was just a black shape against a black sky. For the first time in two years.

Something cold went down my back. Like missing a step on the stairs in the dark.

I dropped a gear and started down the grade. And about halfway down, I saw it.

Off to my right. Down below the four-lane, where the old road used to be. A light. Flashing through the trees and the fog.

Two short. One long.

Then a pause. And again. Two short. One long.

Now, I know what you're thinking. A hunter with a flashlight. Kids messing around. I thought that too. But there's no way down there at night. The old road is cut off at both ends. It's a mile of broken blacktop on the side of a cliff, full of fallen trees.

And that light wasn't a flashlight. It was low and yellow and wide. Like the headlights of an old truck.

My father had a rule. Always leave room to stop. I've been hearing it in my head for most of my life. And that night I did something I've never done before or since. I pulled onto the runaway truck ramp at the bottom of the grade, and I set my brakes.

I sat there with the engine idling and the wipers going. The light kept flashing. Two short. One long. Like somebody calling my name without a voice.

I got my big flashlight out of the sleeper, and I climbed over the guardrail.

I want to stop here for a second and ask you something.

A house with no power for twenty-two years. A porch light that only came on for trucks. A son who flashed two short and one long at his mother every night he came home. And on the one night that porch stayed dark, a light down in the hollow. Flashing that same signal at me. Over and over.

You tell me.

What was that light trying to show me?

It took me twenty minutes to get down to the old road. Mud, wet leaves, briars tearing at my jacket. When I finally stood on that broken blacktop, the flashing had stopped. Just fog, and the sound of water running somewhere below me.

I walked along the old road with my flashlight. My boots were loud on the broken blacktop. Somewhere above me, a truck went by on the four-lane, and it sounded a hundred miles away.

Then I smelled it.

Hot brakes. Sharp and burnt, like a pan left on the stove. The smell I told you about. The smell every driver knows in his stomach before he knows it in his head. It was so strong I turned around and shined my light back up the road. I was sure a truck was coming down on top of me.

There was nothing there. There hadn't been a truck on that road in almost thirty years.

I kept walking. Past a guardrail post leaning like a drunk. Past a sign so rusted you couldn't tell what it used to say. Then the road curved. The same hard curve that's at the bottom of the grade today. And on the outside of that curve, there was no guardrail at all. Just a gap. And past the gap, a steep slope covered in rhododendron so thick you couldn't push your arm through it.

I shined my light down into it.

About forty feet down, something reflected back at me. Round. Glass.

A headlight.

I won't tell you everything I saw. I'll just tell you it was the front of an old cab-over truck, lying on its side. A tree was growing right up through where the windshield used to be. It was green with moss. The bushes had closed over it like a fist. You could have walked past it a thousand times and never seen it.

Thirty-one years, it had been lying there. Forty feet from the road everybody searched.

I climbed back up and called the sheriff from my truck. Then I called my dispatcher and told him I'd be late. He asked me why. I couldn't find the words.

They brought Danny Ferris up the mountain that week. The plates matched. His logbook was in the glove box, swollen and stained, but you could still read his last line. He'd stopped for fuel sixty miles south. Saturday, January nineteenth, nineteen eighty-five.

They buried him next to his mother, in a little church cemetery behind the diner. Bonnie called me so I could come. I did.

A lot of drivers came. Men I'd flashed my lights at for two years and never met. Some of them had driven that hill since the eighties. Some of them had searched that mountain for Danny in the snow. One old man in a feed cap stood next to me the whole time and didn't say a word. At the end, he shook my hand and said, "Thank you for stopping."

We parked our trucks along the road by the church. And when they lowered him down, every one of us flashed our headlights. Two short. One long.

Here's the part I can't explain. I've stopped trying.

When the deputy climbed down to that truck the next morning, he called up to me.

"Hank," he said, "you told me it was flashing?"

"It was," I said.

"The headlight switch is pulled all the way out," he said. "It's on. And this battery's been dead for thirty years."

And the porch light on the Ferris place?

It never came on again. Not for me. Not for anybody. I drove that hill for another year after that. Dark, every single time.

I still have that fuel log. In the back, there are two hundred and six little marks. The last one is dated April nineteenth. Tuesday. Three nights before the porch went dark.

Like she finally saw him come over the hill.

So here's what I'll leave you with.

Always tell somebody your route. Where you're going, which road you're taking, and when you'll call. Danny told his mother he'd be home by one. If he'd told her which road, they might have found him in a day.

And if you've got somebody out on the road tonight, leave a light on for them. They see it. Believe me, they see it.

Next time, I've got another one from the road.

And now I want to hear from you. Did somebody in your family ever leave a light on for you? Or did you leave one on for someone? Tell me in the comments. I read every one.

Drive safe out there.

And if you ever come over a hill at night and a porch light comes on… flash back.
