## 1. SCRIPT (озвучувати як є)

The app said my father's truck was doing forty-four miles an hour.

I was sitting in it.

The truck was parked in my mother's barn. The barn doors were shut. There were no batteries in it. I pulled them out myself, two years before. The engine was stone cold. There were two years of dust on the windshield, and I could see my own breath.

And on the phone in my hand, a little blue dot was moving down a dark county road. Curve after curve. Forty-four miles an hour.

Then the dot stopped.

And my phone rang. It was my son. And the first thing he said was…

Hey. It's Hank.

Tonight I'm parked at a truck stop outside Flagstaff, Arizona. Snow is coming down off the mountain, the heater is ticking, and the coffee in this place tastes like it was made on Tuesday. Still better than no coffee.

Last time I told you about an old man with a cardboard suitcase who paid me ten thousand dollars for a ride home. A lot of you asked if I ever spent that money. I'll tell you one day.

Tonight's story is different. It didn't happen in the nineties. It happened two years ago. It's about my dad's truck. A truck that hadn't moved in two years… and drove thirty-eight miles every single night anyway.

It was November 2024. A Tuesday. I was forty-seven years old, and I was sitting in a truck stop in Amarillo, Texas, at a quarter to two in the morning, with a load of insulation headed west to Phoenix. Cold, dry wind. The kind that finds every gap in the door seals.

Let me tell you about the truck first.

My father, Ray, died on March 9, 2010. He was fifty-nine. He left behind my mother, my sister and me, a shoebox full of fuel receipts, and a green 1979 Kenworth W900. Long nose. Chrome stacks. A seat that hissed like a tired old dog every time he climbed in.

Nobody could sell that truck. My mother wouldn't hear about it. So it lived at her place in southern Ohio, behind the house, in the old barn.

For a few years I took it out when I was home. My son learned to drive a stick shift in it, in an empty church parking lot, with my father's sunglasses still clipped to the visor. My dad used to call him "bud." When my son was little, Dad would sit him on his knee behind that big wheel and say, "Where are we going today, bud?"

In the fall of 2022, the fuel pump started leaking, and I parked it. I pulled both batteries, because somebody in the county had been stealing batteries out of farm trucks. I chained the barn doors. And I did one more thing. I stuck a little GPS tracker under the dash. Thirty dollars online. It runs on its own battery, and if the truck moves, it sends a message to an app on my phone.

I put it there for thieves. For two years, that app said the same thing every day. Parked. Not moving.

Now, if you never drove a truck, here's something you might not know.

These days, almost every truck on the road is being watched. Since the end of 2017, most trucks have to carry what we call an ELD. An electronic logging device. It plugs into the engine and records when the truck moves, how fast it goes, and how long the driver has been behind the wheel. Before that, we kept paper logbooks. One page a day, four lines. Off duty, sleeper berth, driving, on duty. You drew your whole day with a pencil and a ruler.

Today, dispatch can look at a screen and see exactly where you are. Right now. This minute.

But there's an exception. Trucks with engines built before the year 2000 don't have to carry an ELD. Those old engines don't have a computer to plug into. So my dad's '79 never had one. That truck never knew where it was. And my father loved that.

"Nobody's watching, Hank," he used to say. "Just you and the road."

That's why I had to stick that cheap tracker under the dash. It was the only thing in that truck that knew where it was.

And a tracker like that is simple. It doesn't guess. It doesn't dream. It talks to the satellites, it gets a position, and it sends it out. If the truck sits still, the dot sits still. If the dot says the truck went somewhere… then the truck went somewhere.

That's what I thought, anyway.

So. Amarillo. A quarter to two in the morning, Texas time. I was about to climb into the sleeper when my phone buzzed on the dash.

Movement detected.

I almost laughed. Thieves. Two years of nothing, and now somebody finally found the barn. I opened the app, ready to call the sheriff back home.

But the dot wasn't going toward the highway. Thieves go to the highway. This dot turned the other way, onto Old Furnace Road. That's a narrow county road that winds through the hills behind my mother's place. No lines painted on it. Trees right up to the edge.

It was a quarter to three in the morning in Ohio. Two forty-seven, to be exact. I remember the number because I'd see it again.

I watched that dot go. Forty-four on the straights. Slowing down for every curve. Not like a thief in a hurry. Like somebody who knew the road. Nineteen miles out, it stopped at a spot called Miller's Bend, where the road drops down a hill and crosses Salt Lick Creek.

It sat there for eleven minutes.

Then it turned around and came back. Nineteen miles. Into the barn. Parked.

I called my mother at seven, her time. She was seventy-two then, and she walked out to that barn in her robe and her rubber boots, with the phone against her ear.

"Hank," she said, "the chain's on the door. Your daddy's truck is right where you left it. There's dust all over the windshield. There's a spider web from the mirror to the door frame, and the spider's still home."

A glitch, I told myself. Cheap tracker. Cheap satellites.

The next night, at two forty-seven, my phone buzzed again.

Same road. Same speed. Same stop at Miller's Bend. Eleven minutes. Then home.

And the night after that. And the night after that.

On the fourth day, I called the tracker company. A young guy answered, very polite. I explained it. He went quiet for a second.

"Sir," he said, "GPS drift is a few hundred feet. It doesn't make a thirty-eight-mile trip on a real road, with real curves, at real speeds. If the app says it drove… it drove."

I sat there in my cab and I looked at that route for a long time. Every curve of Old Furnace Road. The top of the hill. The bend. The creek.

And I knew that road. I knew it the way you know your own kitchen in the dark.

That was the road my father taught me to drive on.

So tell me. Why would a dead man's truck drive that road every night? And why did it always stop at the same spot… for exactly eleven minutes?

Here's the thing about this job nobody tells you when you start.

You're never home when it happens. Whatever it is. Your kid's first game. Your mother's fall on the ice. The night the furnace quits. You're always eleven hundred miles away, in a parking lot, looking at it on a little screen. Your whole family lives inside that phone. Pictures. Messages. Missed calls.

My son grew up on that screen. I watched him learn to walk on video, in a truck stop in Nebraska. I'm not proud of it. It's just the job.

And that week, the little screen was showing me my father. Every night. Driving a road he hadn't driven in fourteen years.

I couldn't sleep. I couldn't eat. I swapped loads with a driver going east, and I got home on Friday.

On Friday night, I did what anybody would do. I drove the route myself.

I took my pickup, and I left the barn at two forty-five. It had been raining all evening. Wet leaves all over the road, slick as soap. I drove Old Furnace Road slow, curve by curve, with the wipers going and the heater on high.

At the top of the hill before Miller's Bend, I stopped. I didn't plan to. My foot just did it.

Because that's where he taught me.

I was sixteen. My dad made me stop right there, at the top of that hill, and he pointed down at the curve.

"You can't see around that bend," he said. "You don't know what's down there. A deer. A kid on a bicycle. A tractor with no lights. So you drive slow enough to stop for whatever it is. Always leave room to stop, Hank."

That was the first time I ever heard him say it. Later it became his rule for everything. Money. Marriage. Arguments. Always leave room to stop.

I drove down to the bend. There was nothing there. Just the guardrail, a little gap where a post was missing, and the creek running loud and high with the rain.

I sat there for eleven minutes. I felt like a fool. Then I drove home.

And here's the part I couldn't explain. That night, for the first time in a week, the tracker didn't buzz. The truck stayed in the barn.

On Saturday, I made up my mind. If something was going to happen in that truck, I'd be in it.

I unchained the barn at midnight and pulled the doors shut behind me. I brought a blanket and a thermos of coffee. I climbed up into the cab, and without thinking about it, I sat in the passenger seat. The same seat I sat in when I was eight years old.

It was cold. Rain on the tin roof. The chain clinking against the door every time the wind pushed it. Mice somewhere in the hay.

Midnight. One o'clock. Two.

Two forty-seven.

My phone buzzed in my hand.

Movement detected.

I looked at the screen. The dot was leaving the barn. Turning onto Old Furnace Road. Picking up speed.

And the truck around me didn't move an inch.

But the driver's seat did.

That old seat let out a long, slow hiss. The exact sound it made for thirty years whenever my father climbed in. And the cushion pressed down. Like somebody heavy had just settled in behind the wheel.

Then I smelled it. Black coffee. And wintergreen. My dad chewed wintergreen gum his whole life, because the doctor made him quit cigarettes in 1991.

I'm not going to tell you I saw anything. I didn't look. I was forty-seven years old, and I sat there like a little boy, staring straight ahead through a dusty windshield at the barn wall. Holding my phone. Watching that blue dot drive my father's road.

Forty-four on the straights. Slowing for every curve.

At three thirteen, the dot stopped at Miller's Bend.

At three fourteen, my phone rang. It was my son.

"Dad," he said. "I'm okay. I'm okay. I went off the road at Miller's Bend. Somebody pulled me out."

Now. I need to tell you something I didn't know that night.

Eight days earlier, my son had started a new job. Night shift at a warehouse on the other side of the hills. He clocked out at a quarter to three. And to get home, he took the shortcut. Old Furnace Road. Through Miller's Bend.

He didn't tell me. Why would he? He was twenty-six.

I was out of that barn before he finished talking. I didn't even close the doors. I jumped in my pickup and I drove those nineteen miles in the rain.

Every part of me wanted to put my foot through the floor.

But I didn't. Because I could hear him. Plain as day. Leave room to stop, Hank. So I drove like he taught me. And at the top of the hill before the bend, I came around a curve, and there was a deer standing in the middle of the road. A big doe. Eyes shining in the headlights.

I stopped. With room to spare.

She looked at me for a second, and then she walked into the trees.

At the bottom of the hill, my son's little car was lying on its side in Salt Lick Creek. Headlights still on, under the water, glowing green. It had gone right through the gap in the guardrail.

And my son was sitting on the bank, in the mud, wrapped in a heavy old wool coat. Green and black plaid.

I knew that coat. I'd know it anywhere.

I got down on my knees in the mud and I grabbed him, and he was shaking, and he was talking fast, the way people do after a scare.

A deer jumped out, he said. He hit the brakes on the wet leaves and the car just slid. Through the rail, down the bank, into the creek. The car landed on the driver's side. The water came in fast, through the window, cold as ice. His seat belt jammed. He couldn't reach the buckle. The water was at his chin.

"And then the door above me opened, Dad. The passenger door. Somebody reached in. Big rough hands. He cut the belt with a pocketknife and pulled me out like I weighed nothing."

The man carried him up the bank. Put that coat around him. And then he sat down behind my son in the mud and held him. Both arms around him. So he wouldn't shake so hard. So he wouldn't pass out.

"He kept talking to me," my son said. "Low voice. Kind of rough. He kept calling me 'bud.' He said, 'Your dad's on his way, bud. He'll be careful. He always leaves room to stop.'"

My son looked at me.

"Dad. That's your thing. You always say that."

I couldn't answer him.

"When your headlights came over the hill," he said, "the man said, 'There he is.' And he got up and walked up to the road. And I heard a big diesel up there. Idling. Then it pulled away. An old truck, Dad. You know that sound. It sounded just like Grandpa's."

Now I want to ask you something. And I want you to really think about it.

A truck with no batteries, chained in a barn. A dot on a map that drove the same road for eight nights, and stopped every night at three thirteen, at the exact spot where my son drove past on his way home. A man who called him "bud." A coat I hadn't seen since my father's funeral.

You tell me.

Who do you think was holding my son?

The ambulance came about ten minutes later. My son had a broken wrist and he was cold to the bone, but he was okay. The doctors said another few minutes in that water and it would've been a different story. I didn't need the doctors to tell me that.

I sat by his bed until the sun came up. And somewhere around six in the morning, I finally did the math.

The first night that tracker buzzed was the night of my son's first shift. Every night he worked, he drove through Miller's Bend at about three thirteen. Every night he worked, my father's truck was waiting there for him.

And the one night it didn't move? Friday. The night I drove the route myself.

My son had Friday off.

When I got back to my mother's place that morning, I went straight to the barn. The doors were standing open, the way I left them. The truck was right where it had always been. Dust on the windshield. Cold engine. No batteries.

But the spider web from the mirror to the door frame was broken.

The tires were wet. And packed in the tread, all the way around, was gray gravel from the bottom of Salt Lick Creek.

I never asked my mother where Dad's coat had been all those years. I didn't need to. My son still has it. He says it smells like coffee and wintergreen, and no matter how many times he washes it, it doesn't go away.

There's one more thing. And I still can't explain it.

I opened the app that morning and looked at the trip history from that last night. Every other night, there were two trips. Out to the bend, and back home.

That night, there was only one.

Trip ended, three thirteen a.m. Miller's Bend.

According to the satellites, my father's truck never came home that night.

And it has never moved again.

So here's what I'll leave you with.

If somebody you love drives at night, ask them to text you when they get home. Every time. It takes four seconds. My son sends me one word every morning, wherever I am. "Home."

And wherever you're driving, on a road or in your life, leave room to stop. You never know what's around the bend. Sometimes it's a deer. Sometimes it's your kid.

Next time, I'll tell you about a weigh station inspector who had a little business on the side. Every driver who came through his scale paid a fine. And the money never went to the state. For years, nobody could touch him. Until one day he picked the wrong driver.

And now I want to hear from you. Did something that belonged to someone you lost ever keep working after they were gone? A clock, a radio, a phone that rang one more time? Tell me in the comments. I read every one.

Drive safe out there.

And if your phone ever buzzes at two forty-seven in the morning… maybe somebody's just checking on you.

## 2. Нотатки для озвучки

- Темп спокійний, ~150 слів/хв. Три крапки (…) = пауза 1 с. Порожній рядок між абзацами = пауза 0,5 с.
- Гачок (до "Hey. It's Hank.") — тихіше і повільніше за решту. Останнє слово гачка "was…" обірвати, не опускаючи інтонацію.
- Рядки, які сказати найповільніше: "And the truck around me didn't move an inch. / But the driver's seat did." · "Who do you think was holding my son?" · "According to the satellites, my father's truck never came home that night."
- Слова тата (Ray) — трохи нижче і м'якше, не "граючи" інший голос.
- Музика: тихий ambient / піано з аудіобібліотеки YouTube, −24…−28 dB під голосом. На "Movement detected" — коротка тиша без музики.
