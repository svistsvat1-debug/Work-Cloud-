PART A. WHAT WORKS ON THIS CHANNEL (real YouTube Studio data, October 2026)
=====================================================================
Shorts bring 95% of all views (about 1,190 Shorts views vs 54 long-video views). Long videos get 1-5 views each because almost nobody crosses from a Short to the long episode. Everything you create must move Shorts viewers into the long episode.

Top Shorts and why they worked:
1. "Why Does Dad's GPS Still Move?" - 630 views. A dead father's everyday device does something impossible, told with exact numbers ("hasn't moved in two years", "every night at 2:47 a.m.", "the same 38 miles"). Title is a question. Retention above 60-80%.
2. "It Followed Me 300 Miles. Nobody Was Driving" - 110 views in 2 days. Road paradox in two short sentences: a fact, then the fact that makes it impossible.
3. "A Voice on Channel 19 Told Me to Get Off the Road" - 99 views. A warning from nowhere on the CB radio.
4. "My Dad Hid This For 15 Years" - 68 views, over 72% stayed to watch. A hidden object reveals a family secret.
5. "The Waitress Brought 2 Coffees" - 55 views. A small everyday detail that means a dead person is present.

What did NOT work:
- Copying the winner's wording without a new paradox: "Why Is Dad's Toll Pass Still Charging?" (11), "Why Is Dad Still Writing In His Logbook?" (28), "Why Is Dad's Truck Tank Always Empty?" (39). The theme alone does not win. The strength of the specific paradox and its concrete numbers wins.
- "#shorts" inside the title (those Shorts averaged about 21 views, the ones without it about 159).
- A Short and its long episode telling different facts. Viewers who click through must find exactly what the Short promised.
- Hooks that describe a situation instead of an impossibility ("40 Trucks Showed Up. Nobody Ordered a Thing" - 5 views).

What works in the wider niche (competitors, October 2026):
- Titles built as a paradox of two short sentences ("I Heard My Wife Calling Me From the Woods. She Was on the Phone.").
- Calm, intimate night narration; dark, rainy, almost colorless visuals with one warm light; one location per story.

PART D. RULES FOR TEXT THAT A VOICE BOT READS (all scripts, long and short)
=====================================================================
1. Only words Hank speaks. No headings, labels, speaker names, brackets, parentheses, asterisks, stage directions, sound cues, emojis, hashtags or notes inside the script code blocks.
2. Numbers as spoken words: "two forty-seven in the morning", "nineteen ninety-eight", "two hundred eighty dollars", "thirty-eight miles", "forty-four miles an hour".
3. No abbreviations: "in the morning" instead of a.m., "Interstate Eighty" instead of I-80, "miles an hour" instead of mph, "Highway Forty" instead of Hwy 40. "CB" and "Channel nineteen" are fine.
4. Punctuation for rhythm only: a period is a short pause, a blank line between paragraphs is a longer pause, "…" is a long dramatic pause (at most once every 150 words). No em dashes, no semicolons, no colons, no ALL CAPS words.
5. Short spoken sentences: average 8-12 words, never more than 20. Many sentences of 2-5 words at tense moments.
6. Dialogue in plain double quotes, always attributed ("he said", "my mother said"). No more than three lines of dialogue in a row.
7. Names that are easy to pronounce.
8. Plain American spoken English. No profanity.
9. Each script code block holds at most 4,500 characters and is cut only at a paragraph break, so one block fits one request to the voice generator.

PART E. LONG EPISODE STRUCTURE (2,600-3,200 words, about 18-21 minutes)
=====================================================================
In this order (approximate words):
1. COLD HOOK (80-110): the strongest moment, no explanation, the paradox with exact numbers, last sentence cut off with "…".
2. "Hey. It's Hank." Where he is tonight in 2026 (a truck stop, the weather, one small detail like the coffee), one sentence about the previous episode, one sentence restating tonight's paradox (100-130).
3. Exact date and context: year, weekday, hour, state, highway, cargo, Hank's age (130-170).
4. One real detail of trucking life explained for people who never drove a truck, true for that year, which later matters to the plot (250-320).
5. Escalation 1: the impossible thing repeats, a sound or physical detail, ends with an open question to the viewer (500-600).
6. Hank's thoughts: one quiet paragraph about loneliness, fatigue or family (120-170).
7. Turn and escalation 2: something changes the meaning of the beginning (480-560).
8. Peak: a rhetorical question to the viewer that lists the facts, then "You tell me." (120-170).
9. Resolution: answer the main question (300-360).
10. One detail left unexplained, stated plainly (90-130).
11. Practical advice in two or three sentences the listener can use in real life (90-120).
12. Outro: teaser of the next day's long episode from the plan, one comment question specific to this story, "I read every one.", "Drive safe out there." and one final short line about this story (110-140).
A new open question at least every 600 words. Sound and touch details in quiet moments (knocks, hiss of air brakes, CB squelch, cold metal, coffee smell). The story must work for someone who has never seen another episode.
