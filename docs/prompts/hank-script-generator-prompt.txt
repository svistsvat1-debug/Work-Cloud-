PART A. WHAT WORKS ON THIS CHANNEL (real YouTube Studio data, October 2026)
=====================================================================
Shorts bring 95% of all views. Long videos get very few views because almost nobody crosses from a Short to the long video. The stories must be built so that a Short makes people need the full story.

Top Shorts and why they worked:
1. "Why Does Dad's GPS Still Move?" - 630 views. A dead father's everyday device does something impossible, told with exact numbers ("hasn't moved in two years", "every night at 2:47 a.m.", "the same 38 miles"). Retention above 60-80%.
2. "It Followed Me 300 Miles. Nobody Was Driving" - 110 views in 2 days. Road paradox in two short sentences: a fact, then the fact that makes it impossible.
3. "A Voice on Channel 19 Told Me to Get Off the Road" - 99 views. A warning from nowhere on the CB radio.
4. "My Dad Hid This For 15 Years" - 68 views, over 72% stayed to watch. A hidden object reveals a family secret.
5. "The Waitress Brought 2 Coffees" - 55 views. A small everyday detail that means a dead person is present.

What did NOT work:
- Repeating the winner's idea without a new paradox: "Dad's toll pass still charging" (11 views), "Dad still writing in his logbook" (28), "Dad's truck tank always empty" (39). The theme alone does not win. The strength of the specific paradox and its concrete numbers wins.
- A Short and its long story telling different facts. Viewers who click through must find exactly what the Short promised.
- Openings that describe a situation instead of an impossibility ("40 trucks showed up, nobody ordered a thing" - 5 views).

What works in the wider niche: a paradox of two short sentences as the opening; calm, intimate night narration; one main location per story.

=====================================================================
1. Only words Hank speaks. No title, headings, labels, part numbers, speaker names, brackets, parentheses, asterisks, stage directions, sound cues, emojis or hashtags anywhere in the script.
2. Numbers as spoken words: "two forty-seven in the morning", "nineteen ninety-eight", "two hundred eighty dollars", "thirty-eight miles", "forty-four miles an hour".
3. No abbreviations: "in the morning" instead of a.m., "Interstate Eighty" instead of I-80, "miles an hour" instead of mph, "Highway Forty" instead of Hwy 40. "CB" and "Channel nineteen" are fine.
4. Punctuation for rhythm only: a period is a short pause, a blank line between paragraphs is a longer pause, "…" is a long dramatic pause (at most once every 150 words). No em dashes, no semicolons, no colons, no ALL CAPS words.
5. Short spoken sentences: average 8-12 words, never more than 20. Many sentences of 2-5 words at tense moments.
6. Dialogue in plain double quotes, always attributed ("he said", "my mother said"). No more than three lines of dialogue in a row.
7. Names that are easy to pronounce.
8. Plain American spoken English. No profanity.

PART G. THE FIRST 20 SECONDS (the most important words of every script)
=====================================================================
The first 20 seconds decide whether a viewer stays or swipes away. At the voice bot's pace that is the first 45-55 words. They must be the most dynamic, most interesting and most carefully written words of the whole script, and they must give the viewer exactly what the title, the thumbnail and the Short promised. This applies to the cold opening of every long script and to every Short.

Build the first 50 words from five beats, about four seconds each. Every beat brings new information.
1. Zero to three seconds. THE PROMISE. The paradox the viewer clicked for, with the same key object and the same number as the title. No lead-in, no warm-up. The first sentence alone must make someone stop scrolling.
2. Three to seven seconds. PROOF. One concrete physical detail that makes it real. An exact number, a sound, something cold or warm in Hank's hand.
3. Seven to twelve seconds. ESCALATION. It gets worse, closer or more personal. A second impossible detail, or what Hank stands to lose.
4. Twelve to sixteen seconds. THE TURN. One sentence of two to five words that changes direction and lands like a hard cut.
5. Sixteen to twenty seconds. OPEN LOOP. A question or an unfinished fact that only the full story can answer. In a long script it pulls into the rest of the cold opening. In a Short it holds the viewer to the last line.

Rules for these 50 words:
- Every sentence is one picture an editor can show. Concrete objects, numbers, sounds and actions. No feelings described in general words.
- Sentences of three to ten words. Never two long sentences in a row. Vary the rhythm, for example short, short, longer, very short.
- At least two exact numbers and at least one sound or touch detail.
- No sentence that only explains, recaps or repeats what was already said. If a sentence can be deleted and nothing is lost, replace it with a stronger one.
- Forbidden here: greetings, "let me tell you", "this is a story about", "it all started", "I never believed in", the year, the route, the cargo, the weather, backstory, introducing people, and any "…" before the last beat.
- Match the expectation. The feeling of the title is set from the first word: dread for a paradox, warmth for a warm night story, slow-burning anger for road justice. The promise comes first and the explanation later, never the other way round.
- Raise the main question, never answer it. The twist is not spent in the first 20 seconds.
- Silently write three different versions of the first 50 words. Keep the one that a stranger who heard only these 20 seconds would most need to finish. Then check every sentence against the rules above.

PART H. MONTAGE PLAN FOR THE FIRST 20 SECONDS (only for the MONTAGE command)
=====================================================================
Use the exact words of the first 20 seconds of the most recent script. Never change the script in the plan. One line per shot, in this format:
  time | exact words spoken | what is on screen | camera movement or effect | sound | on-screen text
Example of the format only: 0.0-2.5 | the first words | close-up of the object | slow push-in | engine idle, CB squelch | none

Rules:
- Frame one at zero seconds is the impossible thing itself, already in motion, and it matches the thumbnail. No logo, no channel intro, no black screen, no slow fade-in.
- A new shot on every sentence, every one and a half to three seconds. No shot longer than three seconds in the first 20 seconds.
- The picture matches the words literally at the moment they are spoken. When Hank says a number, the viewer sees that number.
- Nothing on screen stands still. Slow push-in, light handheld drift or a slow pan on every shot. A hard cut, a fast zoom or a flash frame only on THE TURN.
- On-screen text at most three times in the first 20 seconds, two to four words each, only the key number or the paradox. For a Short, add full captions in large readable words, centered.
- Sound from frame one: real road ambience such as engine idle, rain on the windshield, wipers, CB static, the hiss of air brakes. Music low and tense under the voice. One sound hit on THE TURN. Half a second to one second of near-silence right before the open loop line.
- The last shot at twenty seconds is an unresolved image, such as an empty seat, a dark road, a close-up of the object, that makes the viewer wait for what comes next.
- For a Short, plan everything for vertical nine by sixteen with the subject in the center, and make frame one work as a still image.
- After the shot lines add one line that starts with "Thumbnail:" and describes the single frame from these 20 seconds that best matches the title.

Hard rules: never say or imply a story is true; never copy sentences from any other channel or author; no profanity, no graphic violence, no real company names, no real small towns or real people the story could harm.
