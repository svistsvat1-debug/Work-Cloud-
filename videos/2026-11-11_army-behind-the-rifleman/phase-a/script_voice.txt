July, nineteen forty-four. Normandy. One American rifleman crouches in a muddy foxhole. He is the tip of the spear.

But alone, he would not last long. Behind him stands an army he will never meet. So how many men did it take to keep him fighting?

Let's play it like a game. Your job is to keep him in the fight. Every mission is a real problem the army had to solve. If one fails, he is out. Mission one: feed him.

A rifleman digs, carries and marches all day. But he cannot cook in a hole in the ground. So the army gave him the K-ration. Three small boxes for one day: breakfast, dinner and supper.

Together, they held close to three thousand calories. For men in combat, even that was often not enough. Each meal also came with four cigarettes. The supper box even had chewing gum and toilet paper. One small trick helped too. The outer box was waxed, so many men burned it to heat their coffee.

But after weeks of the same boxes, men were sick of them. Even the emergency chocolate bar tasted bad on purpose. The army asked for it to taste only a little better than a boiled potato. That way, nobody would eat it as a snack.

But food creates a bigger problem. One box weighs almost nothing. Millions of boxes weigh thousands of tons. Somebody has to pack them, count them and haul them forward.

When the front was quiet, cooks tried to send hot food up after dark. So the cook and the supply man join our invisible team. But every box rode on a truck. Every truck needed fuel.

Mission two: move everything. In August nineteen forty-four, the Allies broke out of Normandy and raced across France. But their supplies were still coming ashore in Normandy, over the beaches and through the port of Cherbourg.

Worse, Cherbourg was no gift. The Germans had wrecked its harbor before giving it up. So every mile the tanks moved forward, the supply line grew one mile longer.

The railways could not carry it. Allied bombers had smashed much of the French rail network before the invasion. So the army built a railway out of trucks. They called it the Red Ball Express.

Nearly six thousand trucks ran on one-way loop roads. Convoys rolled day and night. About three out of four drivers were Black soldiers. In a segregated army, most Black soldiers were placed in service units, not combat units. So many of them drove.

Most drivers were under twenty-four, and few had driven a truck before the war. A round trip from Cherbourg could take fifty-four hours. The rules said twenty-five miles per hour. In practice, many raced alone through the blackout at over sixty.

By mid-November, they had delivered about four hundred twelve thousand tons of supplies. But it still was not enough. On August thirtieth, General Patton's Third Army asked for four hundred thousand gallons of gasoline.

It received about thirty-two thousand. So the tanks stopped near the Meuse River and waited. For a few days, empty fuel tanks held them back more than enemy guns.

So the truck driver joins our invisible team. But fuel and food only keep a body going. What keeps his mind going?

Mission three: morale. For a soldier far from home, few things mattered more than a letter. But paper is heavy. One hundred fifty thousand one-page letters filled thirty-seven mail sacks. They weighed more than two thousand five hundred pounds.

So the army shrank them. From June nineteen forty-two, Victory Mail letters were photographed onto tiny rolls of film. The same hundred fifty thousand letters now fit in one sack, weighing about forty-five pounds.

But by early nineteen forty-five, a new problem appeared. Millions of letters and parcels sat stuck in warehouses in Birmingham, England. Many were for soldiers who had moved or been wounded. Others were badly addressed, so someone had to play detective.

So the army sent in the Six Triple Eight. Eight hundred fifty-five Black women of the Women's Army Corps, led by Major Charity Adams. It was the only Black women's battalion sent overseas in the war. They were told the job would take six months.

They worked in three shifts, seven days a week, in cold and badly lit buildings. Each shift handled around sixty-five thousand pieces of mail. They cleared the backlog in three months. Their motto was short: No mail, low morale.

So the mail clerk joins our invisible team. But some letters came back unopened, because the man they were written for had been hit.

Mission four: keep him alive when he is wounded. Every minute counts, and here the invisible army moves fastest. First comes the company medic, with bandages and morphine. But he cannot operate in a hedgerow.

So litter bearers carry the man back to a battalion aid station, often about a kilometer behind the line. The goal was treatment within about an hour. From there, ambulances take him to a clearing station, where surgeons can operate. The worst cases go on to bigger hospitals.

Every step needs people: doctors, nurses, drivers, and clerks who track every patient. Add blood plasma and new drugs like penicillin. In the First World War, about eight of every hundred wounded Americans died of their wounds. In Europe in this war, it was closer to four.

So the medic joins our invisible team. But medicine could not save everyone. Mission five is the hardest. Sometimes a rifleman did not come back.

Even then, the army still had work to do. Graves Registration units searched the battlefield for every fallen soldier. Each man wore two identity tags. One stayed with him. The other went to the records, so his family would be told. After the war, families got a choice: bring him home, or let him rest overseas.

More than one hundred seventy-one thousand were brought home. Others stayed. Today, nine thousand three hundred eighty-eight Americans rest in the cemetery above Omaha Beach. So the graves clerk joins our invisible team, making sure no one is forgotten. But the front was running out of men.

Mission six: replace him. No matter how well he was fed, supplied and healed, riflemen were lost faster than anyone else. By December nineteen forty-four, commanders in Europe expected to be short about twenty-three thousand riflemen.

The army had plenty of soldiers. It did not have enough infantrymen. So it turned to its own tail. In September and October alone, over fourteen thousand replacements trained for other jobs were retrained as riflemen.

But in December, the Germans struck in the Ardennes. In the chaos of the Battle of the Bulge, cooks, clerks and drivers grabbed rifles to hold the line.

So the army in Europe tried something new. It asked Black soldiers in service units to volunteer as riflemen. Thousands stepped forward. Many sergeants gave up their stripes, just for the chance to fight.

By February, four thousand five hundred sixty-two had volunteered. Many fought as extra platoons inside white rifle companies. It was an early crack in a segregated army.

So the retrained rifleman joins our team. He was never meant for the front, until the front needed him. But what about the enemy's own tail?

Mission seven: look at the other side. In newsreels, the German army looked like a machine of tanks and trucks. But most German divisions moved on foot. Their guns and supplies were often pulled by horses.

It started the war with about five hundred thousand horses. Over the whole war, the German army used roughly two and three quarter million horses and mules. Horses need food, rest and vets of their own. So the German supply line had a hungry, tired tail too.

Both sides learned the same hard truth. A soldier at the front is only as strong as the line behind him. So now we can answer the question.

How many men did it take to keep one rifleman fighting? Look at First Army, the American army that landed in Normandy.

By D-Day, only about fourteen percent of First Army was infantry. Just eight percent served in tanks. Almost forty-five percent were support troops: supply, maintenance, ordnance and medical.

The rest fired the artillery, built bridges and strung telephone wire. Now do the math. For every infantryman in that army, there were about six other soldiers.

Six men he would mostly never meet. Some of them were on our team: the cook, the driver, the mail clerk, the medic. Others fixed engines, cleared roads and kept the radios working. But here is the twist. That small slice at the front paid the biggest price.

Of all the American ground soldiers killed in battle in World War Two, about three out of four were infantrymen. So the answer has two sides.

In First Army, it took about seven soldiers to put one infantryman in the line. Yet that one man carried most of the danger for all seven. Remember the tanks waiting at the Meuse? When the line behind broke, even the strongest front stopped. Battles were won at the front. But wars were won by the long, invisible line behind it.
