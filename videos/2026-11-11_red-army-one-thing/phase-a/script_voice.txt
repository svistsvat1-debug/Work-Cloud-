Every day, a Red Army soldier did dozens of jobs to stay alive. He dug. He marched. He wrapped his feet in rags. But one tiny job, ten seconds with a pencil, many of them refused to do. Decades later, people still dig in forests because of it.

So let's live one ordinary day with him. Not a battle. Just a day. Because most of his war was not shooting. It was everything in between.

Four in the morning, somewhere on the Eastern Front, nineteen forty-three. Our soldier wakes up in a zemlyanka. That is a dugout. A hole in the ground, roofed with logs and earth. In winter, a small stove fights the cold. In spring, the floor turns to mud. Several men sleep here, shoulder to shoulder, in their coats.

But who is he? Not simply a Russian. The Red Army drew men from across the whole Soviet Union. He could be Ukrainian, Kazakh, Georgian, Belarusian or Russian. Some soldiers were women. Hundreds of thousands of them.

His first job of the day is his feet. He has no socks. Instead, he has portyanki. Two rectangles of cloth, wrapped tightly around each foot before the boot goes on.

Sounds miserable. But there was a logic to it. A wet sock stays wet. A foot wrap can be unwound, turned around, and wrapped again with the dry end on the toes. For a man who walked everywhere, that mattered. The idea lasted a long time. The Russian army only started replacing foot wraps with socks in twenty thirteen.

But dry feet will not feed him. And breakfast had a strange problem.

Breakfast came in the dark. Why? Because a field kitchen makes smoke. And smoke on the horizon tells the enemy's artillery exactly where to aim.

So the kitchens cooked before dawn or after sunset, a few kilometers behind the line. The food could not come to the soldiers on wheels. So men carried it.

They hauled pots and thermoses forward through the trenches, under fire, often at night. Some of them never came back with the soup.

In the ideal plan, a soldier got hot food twice a day. Once before dawn. Once after dark. In practice, front-line men often ate once a day. The rest was dry bread, crackers, and whatever was in a tin.

So he has eaten. The sun is coming up. What does a soldier do with a whole day when nobody is attacking?

He digs. Every time a unit stopped, the men dug. A hole to lie in. A trench to move in. A shelter for the night.

His small spade was not a spare tool. It was the one thing between him and every shell that landed nearby.

Look at Kursk, in the summer of nineteen forty-three. Before the battle, soldiers and civilian workers dug defensive lines on a scale that is hard to imagine. Thousands of kilometers of trenches, in belt after belt, across one region.

That was not done by machines. It was done by people like him, one spade at a time.

But a trench does not move. And the Red Army was about to spend two years moving west. First, though, every soldier had to survive a season that no trench could stop.

But there was a problem the spade could not solve. Winter. On the Eastern Front, the cold could kill as surely as a shell. So his day changed with the season.

His greatcoat was not just a coat. At night, it was his blanket. In winter, many soldiers wore a quilted jacket called a telogreika, and boots made of pressed felt, called valenki.

In the first winter of the war, outside Moscow, that gear mattered. Many German units had arrived without proper winter clothing. Soviet units fighting there were often better dressed for the cold.

But warm clothes do not carry shells. And as the front moved, the real question became: how do you supply an army that is always walking?

So he walks. Most of the Red Army's infantry crossed Eastern Europe on foot. With a rifle, a rolled greatcoat, a gas mask bag, and that spade.

But here is the twist. The trucks that rolled past him, carrying his shells and his bread, were often American.

Through Lend-Lease, the United States sent the Soviet Union hundreds of thousands of trucks and jeeps. The most famous was the Studebaker.

He even ate American food. Soldiers got tins of American canned meat. And they gave it a sarcastic nickname. They called it the second front.

Because for years, they had been waiting for the Allies to open a real second front in Western Europe. That front only opened in June nineteen forty-four. The tins arrived long before it.

So his body is moving and fed. But what kept a man going, day after day?

For many, the answer came in a small cup. On the twenty-second of August, nineteen forty-one, the State Defense Committee signed decree number five hundred sixty-two.

It gave front-line troops one hundred grams of forty-percent vodka a day. Soldiers called it the People's Commissar's hundred grams. The idea was not new. In January nineteen forty, during the war with Finland, a similar ration had been proposed. One hundred grams of vodka and fifty grams of pork fat, against the cold.

But there was a catch. The ration was not for everyone. It was meant for the men in the first line of battle. And over the next two years, the rules kept changing.

By nineteen forty-three, the daily mass issue was stopped. The hundred grams were kept mainly for units on the attack. Think about what that meant. If the cup came round in the morning, it could mean the day would not be ordinary at all.

But the evening still had to come. And the evening had its own rituals.

In the evening, he smokes. He gets coarse tobacco called makhorka. Cigarette paper is scarce. So he rolls it in whatever paper he can find. Very often, that was a scrap of newspaper.

Then he writes home. He takes one sheet of paper. No envelope. No stamp. He folds it into a triangle, and writes the address on the outside.

Mail from the front was free. A military censor read it, and blacked out anything that could help the enemy.

For a family back home, a triangle in the post was the best news in the world. It meant he had been alive when he wrote it. Families kept them for decades. Thousands of these triangles now sit in museums and archives, still folded the way a soldier folded them.

So every week, he wrote his own name on the outside of a letter. Remember that.

There is one more thing to understand about his day. He could not leave it. Front-line soldiers almost never got home leave.

A political officer watched the unit's mood. In July nineteen forty-two, Order number two hundred twenty-seven declared: not one step back.

Retreating without orders could be treated as treason. Men who broke the rules could be sent to penal units, and given the most dangerous tasks.

So his day had no exit. There was only the next day, and the one after that. Which brings us to night. And to the one job he refused to do.

Around his neck, or in a small pocket, he carried a little capsule. Black, about five centimeters long, with a screw-on lid. The Red Army introduced it in March nineteen forty-one.

Inside was a slip of paper. On it, he was supposed to write his name, the year he was born, and his home address. Ten seconds with a pencil. That was the job. He also had a small Red Army book, a kind of paybook with his details. But books burned, soaked through, or were taken. The capsule was meant to survive when everything else did not.

And many soldiers simply never did it. Search volunteers today say that most capsules they find were never filled in. Why? Superstition.

Many men believed that filling in the slip was like marking yourself for death. Some kept a needle and thread in the capsule instead.

Think about the contrast. This man wrote his name on a letter home every week. But he would not write it on the one piece of paper meant for the day he died.

Look at every other job in his day. The foot wraps. The soup. The digging. The walking. Each one was a way of reaching tomorrow. Writing that slip was the only job that admitted tomorrow might not come.

The cost of that choice is still being counted. Millions of Soviet soldiers are still listed as missing.

Every year, volunteer search teams walk into the forests and fields of the old Eastern Front. They find helmets, buttons, boots. Sometimes they find a capsule. They open it carefully. And very often, there is no name inside. Without a name, a soldier cannot be sent home. He is reburied with others, under a stone that says unknown. His family may have waited their whole lives for a letter that never came.

So what did a Red Army soldier refuse to do? Everything else, he did. He refused to write the end of his own story.
