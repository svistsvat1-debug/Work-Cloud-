In nineteen thirty-seven, one army ordered a chocolate bar that tasted only a little better than a boiled potato. On purpose.

Another army lost more soldiers to hunger and disease than to enemy bullets. So which country fed its soldiers best in World War Two?

Today we rank six armies, from starving to spoiled. And every army gets three scores.

What the ration promised on paper. How it actually tasted. And the big one: did the food ever reach the soldier?

Because a perfect menu means nothing if the truck never arrives.

Number six: the Imperial Japanese Army.

By nineteen forty-two, Japan held a huge empire across Asia and the Pacific. But it could not feed the men holding it.

On paper, the Japanese soldier ate simply. His ration was mostly rice, with dried fish, pickled plums and powdered soy paste.

It was light, so a man could carry several days of food on his back. But the plan had a fatal flaw.

Japanese commanders often expected troops to find food where they fought. So when American submarines sank the supply ships, whole islands ran out of food.

On Guadalcanal, Japanese soldiers gave the island a new name. They called it Starvation Island.

On New Guinea, cut-off units survived on grass, roots and anything they could catch.

Imagine a nineteen-year-old private on a jungle island. His rice bag is empty. The next supply ship is at the bottom of the sea. And nobody is coming.

Japanese historian Yoshida Yutaka estimates that about sixty percent of Japan's military dead never died in battle. They died from starvation and disease.

So Japan finishes last. Not because the rice was bad, but because for over a million men, it never came.

But hunger was not the only way to ruin a soldier's dinner. Some armies had food, and their soldiers still hated it.

Number five: Italy.

Italian soldiers in North Africa lived on a hard biscuit called the galletta, and small tins of boiled beef.

The tins were stamped with two letters, A and M, for the Military Administration.

But soldiers had their own meaning. A popular story says they read it as Asino Morto. Dead donkey.

And when German troops ate the same tins, they had a joke too. Alter Mann. Old man.

So in the desert heat, the same beef and the same biscuits came back, day after day.

And the Italian army had far fewer trucks than the British it was fighting. So food often moved slowly, if it moved at all.

But Italy had one comfort that most armies did not. Its soldiers were entitled to a daily ration of wine.

That small glass lifts Italy above Japan. But a glass of wine was nothing compared to what the next army poured.

Number four: the Soviet Red Army.

Its ration was built on one thing. Bread.

From September nineteen forty-one, a front-line soldier was promised eight hundred grams of rye bread a day. In winter, nine hundred.

That is almost a kilo of heavy black bread, every single day. Bread came first because it was cheap, filling, and could be baked almost anywhere.

On the march, it became hard dried crusts that lasted for weeks. A soldier soaked them in tea or soup, or just chewed for a very long time.

But the Red Army also had a famous extra. Front-line troops were issued one hundred grams of vodka a day.

But the biggest surprise in Soviet mess tins came from America.

Through Lend-Lease, the United States shipped huge amounts of canned meat to the Soviet Union.

Soldiers gave the American cans a sarcastic nickname. They called them the second front.

Because for years, American cans reached Russia long before American soldiers landed in France.

So the Red Army sits in the middle. Plenty of bread, a little vodka, and a lot of borrowed meat.

But its enemy had a ration so precious that soldiers could be punished just for eating it.

Number three: the Wehrmacht, the German army.

Every German soldier carried an emergency ration called the iron portion.

Hard biscuits, a tin of meat, dried vegetables, coffee and salt.

He could only eat it when his commander allowed it. Men who opened it early could be locked up.

Day to day, the army cooked hot meals in mobile field kitchens. Soldiers nicknamed them goulash cannons.

In the good years, this system worked very well. But it depended on trucks, trains and horses, and Russia broke all three.

At Stalingrad, the trapped Sixth Army ran so short of food that it slaughtered its own horses.

For many German soldiers, Christmas nineteen forty-two meant snow, frozen fingers and a thin soup made from horsemeat.

So Germany takes third place. A brilliant plan that collapsed when the roads ran out.

But the next army solved the problem differently. It packed a whole day of food into one wooden box.

Number two: the British Army.

Its secret weapon was the Composite Ration. Soldiers just called it compo.

One crate fed fourteen men for a whole day.

Inside were tins of steak and kidney pudding, Scotch broth, bacon, vegetables and a sweet pudding.

There were biscuits, jam, chocolate and cigarettes too.

But the most British thing in the box was the tea. It came already mixed with sugar and milk powder.

So a soldier only needed hot water to brew up, almost anywhere.

In the desert, in the jungle or in a muddy Normandy hedgerow, a hot brew was the moment a soldier felt human again.

The crates came in different menus marked with letters. But men often got the same menu again and again.

Still, Britain earns second place for comfort, variety and that tea.

But the winner had something the British did not. Money, factories, and a very strange idea of dessert.

Number one: the United States.

Remember that chocolate bar from the start? In nineteen thirty-seven, an army officer named Paul Logan asked Hershey to make it.

He wanted it to taste only a little better than a boiled potato. Because if it tasted good, soldiers would eat it before the emergency.

So the D ration bar was hard, bitter and so dense that men struggled to bite it.

The daily rations were not loved either. Soldiers grumbled about the same canned hash and stew.

So why is America number one? Because it could deliver food almost anywhere on Earth.

A small K ration box held a full meal, with coffee, candy, gum and cigarettes.

For small groups, there was the ten-in-one ration. One box fed ten men for a whole day.

Behind the lines, field kitchens served hot meals, and on Thanksgiving, many units even got turkey.

But the real proof was dessert.

In nineteen forty-five, the United States Navy spent about one million dollars on a concrete barge with one job.

It made ice cream. About ten gallons every seven minutes.

It was towed to the Pacific to serve sailors on ships without their own ice cream machines.

So the country that made chocolate taste bad on purpose also floated an ice cream factory across an ocean.

That is the gap between starving and spoiled. On one island, a soldier ate grass. Across the same ocean, a sailor ate ice cream.

And the difference was not taste. It was ships, trucks and factories.

So here is the final ranking. Japan, Italy, the Soviet Union, Germany, Britain, and at the very top, the United States.
