Picture a World War Two sailor. You probably see him firing a huge gun at enemy planes. But on most days, his guns never fired a single shot. So what did three hundred men on one small steel ship actually do all day? Let's spend one day aboard and find out. By the end, you'll see why the most boring part of that day saved their lives.

Your day doesn't start in the morning. It starts in the middle of the night. You're a young sailor on an American destroyer in the Pacific, in nineteen forty-four. Sailors called these ships tin cans, because their steel skin was thin and they rolled with every wave.

Somebody shakes your bunk. It's a quarter to four in the morning, and your watch begins at four. The navy split the day into four-hour watches. You stand one watch, then you get eight hours off. But those eight hours are full of work. So a whole night of sleep is something you simply stop having.

Your bed doesn't help. It's a canvas rack, stacked three or four high, in a hot compartment that smells of oil and sweat. So in the tropics, many men dragged their mattresses up and slept on the open deck.

Tonight your job is lookout. You stand on deck with binoculars and stare at black water for four hours. You're searching for a periscope, a wake, a shadow in the sky. Most nights, you see nothing at all. But you can't relax, because the one night you miss something could be the last night for everyone.

At dawn, an alarm rings through the whole ship. General quarters. Every man runs to his battle station. Why every single morning? Because first light was a favorite time for submarines and planes to attack. So the whole crew waits at the guns while the sun comes up.

Usually, nothing happens. The sky stays empty. The alarm stops, and three hundred tired men walk to breakfast. Tomorrow they'll do it again. Tonight at dusk, too.

Breakfast is on a crowded mess deck. You eat fast, standing in line with a steel tray. Coffee is everywhere, strong and black, day and night. But there's no beer to go with it. The United States Navy had banned alcohol on its ships back in nineteen fourteen. So coffee became the fuel that ran the crew.

Now comes the part nobody puts in war movies. Work. The sea is a slow enemy. Salt water attacks every inch of steel, every single day. So sailors spend hours chipping old paint and rust with little hammers. They paint the spot grey again, and the salt comes right back. It never ends.

But there was a twist. Early in the war, the navy learned that thick layers of paint burned like fuel when a ship was hit. So crews were ordered to strip paint from inside their own ships. Imagine scraping paint off your ship in the middle of a war, because the paint itself could kill you.

On top of that, there's cleaning. The navy called a big cleaning day a field day. Decks are scrubbed, brass is polished, and an officer checks every corner for dust.

But a ship in the middle of the ocean has a problem. It needs fresh water and fuel, and there's no shop out there. Fresh water came from machines that boiled sea water. Those machines barely kept up with the boilers and the galley. So water for the men came last. Showers were short, rationed, or done in salt water that left your skin sticky.

Fuel was an even bigger problem. A destroyer could burn through its tanks in a few days. So it had to pull alongside a much bigger ship in open sea and take fuel through hoses. Two ships rolling side by side, only a few dozen meters apart. One wrong turn of the wheel, and they collide.

Running low on fuel was dangerous in another way. In December nineteen forty-four, a typhoon hit the American fleet in the Philippine Sea. Some destroyers were low on fuel, riding high and light in the water. Three of them rolled over and sank. About seven hundred and ninety men were lost in that storm, and the enemy never fired a shot.

After lunch, the alarm rings again. But it's not an attack. It's a drill. Fire drill. Man overboard drill. Gun drill. Damage control drill, where you practice patching holes and fighting fires in dark, flooded rooms.

Sailors complained about drills constantly. They were hot, exhausting, and the same every day. So why practice the same thing a hundred times? Hold on to that question, because the answer comes at the end of this video.

In the evening, the ship slows down a little. This is the best part of the day. Maybe the mail has arrived. Letters could take weeks to reach a ship, so when the mail bags came aboard, the whole crew went quiet.

Many letters arrived as V mail. These were tiny photographed copies of letters, shrunk down to save space on planes. Men read the same letter again and again, until the paper was soft.

No mail? Then there are cards, dice, and a navy board game called acey-deucey. Sometimes a movie plays on a sheet hung across the deck. The same few films played so often that sailors could say every line along with the actors.

There were stranger traditions, too. When a ship crossed the equator, sailors who had never crossed it were called pollywogs. The veterans, called shellbacks, put them through a day of silly tests, costumes and dunking. In the middle of a war, they still made time for a party at sea.

At sunset comes one last rule. Darken ship. Every light on deck goes out. No flashlights, and no smoking outside. Because one glowing cigarette could be seen by a submarine far away. So the whole ship disappears into the night.

And you? You've got the midnight watch. So your day starts again before you've had time to sleep. Watch, alarm, work, drill, letters, watch. Weeks of it. Months of it. But one day was not normal.

April sixteenth, nineteen forty-five. The waters off Okinawa. The destroyer Laffey is far out from the fleet on radar picket duty. Its job is to spot enemy planes early and warn the ships behind it.

That morning, the planes came straight for the Laffey. Dozens of them. Many were kamikazes, pilots who planned to crash their planes into ships. For about eighty minutes, the Laffey was attacked again and again.

It was hit by six kamikaze planes and four bombs. Fires everywhere. The rudder jammed. Compartments flooded. Thirty-two men were killed, and dozens more were wounded. A ship like that should have sunk.

But the Laffey stayed afloat. Why? Because its crew had done all of this before. Not for real, but in drills. Again and again, every afternoon. Every man knew where the hoses were in the dark. Every man knew which door to close and which hole to patch. The boring drills that sailors hated kept their ship alive.

So what did World War Two sailors actually do all day? Mostly, they kept their ship alive. They scraped, cleaned, watched and practiced, day after day. Because a sailor's war was months of boring days, all built for one terrible hour.

The Laffey survived the war. Today, you can still walk on its deck. It's a museum ship in South Carolina.
