Normandy, nineteen forty-four. This American soldier is about to cross one field. He carries a rifle, a shovel, and one day of food.

But he is not alone. Behind him stands a line of people no war movie shows. How long is that line? By the end, you'll know the exact number.

Let's start with something simple. Breakfast. At the front, our soldier often eats a K ration. A small waxed cardboard box of food.

Three boxes make one day. Breakfast, dinner, and supper. Together, they hold about two thousand eight hundred and thirty calories.

Each box was planned down to small details. Besides the food, there were a few cigarettes, chewing gum, and toilet paper. Somebody, in an office far away, decided all of that.

That sounds like plenty. But it isn't. A man who digs holes and carries ammunition all day burns far more. So front-line soldiers were hungry almost all the time.

Now ask a strange question. Who made this box? Not an army cook. A factory, thousands of miles away, back in the United States.

Farmers grew the food. Factory workers packed it. Railroad crews hauled it to a port. Dockworkers lifted it onto a ship.

So before our soldier takes one bite, dozens of people have already touched his breakfast. But food is the easy part. Food does not explode. And food does not sink.

Everything this soldier uses has to cross the Atlantic Ocean. That is more than three thousand miles of open water.

But German submarines, the U-boats, were hunting the ships that carried it. Every ship that went down took bullets, food, and sometimes men with it.

So America needed ships. Not a few. Thousands. The answer was the Liberty ship. A simple, slow cargo ship, built almost like a car on an assembly line.

Many of the welders in those shipyards had never held a torch before the war. Plenty of them were women. They learned in weeks a job that used to take years.

American shipyards built two thousand seven hundred and ten of them. One of them, the Robert E. Peary, went from keel to launch in four days and fifteen hours.

But a ship needs a crew. The men who sailed these cargo ships were mostly civilian merchant mariners. Not soldiers. Yet their job was one of the deadliest at sea.

A ship, in less than a week. That sounds like the problem is solved. But a ship only gets you to the coast. And by the end of summer, our soldier was nowhere near the coast.

By late August nineteen forty-four, the Allied armies had raced across France. The front was hundreds of miles from the beaches. And the French railways had been wrecked by bombing.

So the Army did something simple. It used trucks. Thousands of trucks, driving day and night on one-way roads closed to everyone else. They called it the Red Ball Express.

Most of those trucks were the two-and-a-half-ton workhorse the soldiers called the deuce and a half. Each one was loaded far beyond what it was built for.

On August twenty-ninth, nineteen forty-four, the Red Ball set its record. Five thousand nine hundred and fifty-eight vehicles carried over twelve thousand tons forward. In a single day.

About three out of four of those drivers were Black soldiers. They served in a segregated army. They drove for days with very little sleep.

Some drivers fell asleep at the wheel. Trucks broke down by the thousands. But the convoys kept rolling, because the front was screaming for one thing above all.

There was a cruel twist. The longer the road got, the more gasoline the trucks themselves burned. A big share of the fuel was used up just delivering fuel.

Gasoline. And even this was not enough. At the end of August, General Patton's tanks ground to a halt near the Meuse river. Not because of the Germans. Because the fuel ran out.

So here is our first big clue. Fighting a war is not mostly fighting. It is moving things. And moving things takes people. Lots of people.

But soldiers do not run on gasoline. They run on hope. And at the front, hope usually arrived in an envelope.

Millions of letters crossed the ocean. But paper is heavy. Every sack of mail on a ship was space that could have carried shells or food.

So the Army got clever. Letters were photographed onto tiny rolls of film. They called it Victory Mail, or V mail.

Thirty-seven mail bags of ordinary letters shrank into one single bag of film. On the other side of the ocean, the film was printed back onto paper and delivered.

For a nineteen-year-old in a muddy hole, that letter could be the only proof the world back home still existed. Mothers wrote about the weather. Sisters wrote about weddings. Nobody wrote about the war.

Which means that somewhere behind the front, soldiers spent their whole war printing, sorting, and delivering letters to strangers. Nobody makes movies about them. But every man at the front waited for their work.

Now the hardest moment of the day. Our soldier is wounded. Not badly, but he cannot walk. What happens next?

First, a medic reaches him. He has no hospital. Just a canvas bag and his own two hands. His job is simple and brutal. Buy time.

Litter bearers carry him back. A battalion aid station checks him. An ambulance drives him to a field hospital, where surgeons go to work.

Speed mattered more than anything. A wounded man who reached a surgeon quickly had a far better chance of living. So the whole chain was built to save minutes.

Some wounded men went even further. Back to England. Some all the way home. Every step of that trip is handled by a different group of people.

So let's count who we have met. Farmers. Factory workers. Shipbuilders. Sailors. Truck drivers. Mail clerks. Medics. Surgeons. How many of them stand behind one soldier?

To answer that, the Army used a strange word. A slice. Not of bread. A division slice.

A division is a big army unit. In nineteen forty-four, an American infantry division had a little over fourteen thousand men on paper.

But planners noticed something. Every division in the line needed many more men behind it, in the same theater of war. Just to keep it moving.

The division, plus its share of everyone supporting it, is the division slice. And the slice was much bigger than the division itself. How much bigger? Hold that thought.

Because first, the Army hit a problem nobody expected. By late nineteen forty-four, America had the biggest army in its history. Around eight million people in the Army alone.

But in Europe, generals were running out of one kind of soldier. Not drivers. Not cooks. Not clerks. Riflemen.

Rifle companies were losing men faster than new ones could arrive. Over months of fighting, some infantry divisions lost more men in total than they had on the first day.

So the Army pulled men from other jobs. Anti-aircraft gunners, clerks, and air force trainees were sent to infantry training and handed rifles.

In late December nineteen forty-four, the Army even asked Black service troops to volunteer for combat. Thousands stepped forward. Many sergeants gave up their stripes, just for the chance to fight.

About two thousand two hundred of them went to the front in thirty-seven platoons. So here is the real question. How can an army of eight million run out of riflemen?

Let's do the math. Our soldier is in a rifle company. A rifle company had about one hundred and ninety-three men. An infantry division had twenty-seven of them.

Twenty-seven times one hundred and ninety-three is about five thousand two hundred men. Out of fourteen thousand. So even inside a division, most men were not in rifle companies.

Now zoom out to Europe. In June nineteen forty-five, each American division there came with a slice of about thirty-five thousand five hundred men.

Divide the slice by the men in rifle companies. The answer is almost seven. Almost seven Americans in Europe for every one man in a rifle company.

Six other men in the theater for every rifleman. Drivers, engineers, gunners, mechanics, cooks, clerks, medics. All of them needed, all of them working. But only one of them walks across the field.

But we are not done. Add the air force, the training camps, and everyone in uniform back home. Divide the whole Army by its eighty-nine divisions. That is over ninety thousand men each.

Divide that by the men in rifle companies. About eighteen. For every soldier walking toward the enemy, seventeen others in uniform stood behind him. And that is before we count a single farmer or factory worker.

This was not waste. It is why the American soldier usually had food, ammunition, and letters from home. But the system had a price.

Infantrymen were only about one in seven Americans overseas. Yet they took about seven of every ten casualties. A tiny tip on a giant spear, taking almost every hit.

So the next time you watch a war movie, remember the line behind the soldier. Seventeen people working so one man could cross that field. And one man, carrying the war for all of them.
