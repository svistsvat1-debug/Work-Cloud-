In August nineteen forty-five, Japan still had thousands of hidden warplanes. It still had warships, sailors, and harbors full of mines. Two years later, almost all of it was gone. But in nineteen fifty, a few of its old ships slipped out in secret, heading for a war. So what really happened to Japan's war machine?

On September second, nineteen forty-five, Japan signed the surrender aboard the battleship USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay. The Allies' first demand was simple. Nothing Japanese would ever fly into battle again.

Japan's leaders had been saving those hidden planes for one final battle. They expected an invasion of the home islands. Many of the planes were meant to be flown straight into the invasion fleet. Across Japan, ground crews were now ordered to take the propellers off their own aircraft. Fighters that had been fueled for that last battle sat on the grass like birds with their wings clipped. Many of the young pilots had trained for missions they were never meant to return from. Now they watched the planes they would have died in being dragged into piles.

But the Americans did not destroy everything at once. They wanted to know exactly how these machines were built. So intelligence teams picked out about four examples of each new aircraft type. Those planes were crated, loaded onto ships, and sent across the Pacific to be tested. A few of them still sit in American museums today. The rest were burned, crushed, or cut up for scrap. The work dragged on well into nineteen forty-seven.

Planes were easy to destroy, though. Ships were a different problem, because Japan desperately needed them.

When the war ended, more than six million Japanese were stranded far from home. About half were soldiers, and the other half were civilians. They were scattered from the cold plains of Manchuria to the jungles of New Guinea. Many of them were sick and hungry. They waited on beaches and in camps with nothing but a name on a list.

So the defeated navy was given one last mission. Its surviving warships were stripped of their guns and turned into ferries. Even aircraft carriers joined in.

The carrier Katsuragi had been finished too late to fight. She never sent a single squadron into battle. Now her empty hangars were filled with exhausted men coming home from places like Rabaul. The old carrier Hosho became a passenger ship too. She was one of the first ships in the world designed as a carrier from the keel up. For a soldier who had not seen Japan in years, the first view of home came from a warship. It was a warship that had lost its war.

But this mission could not last forever. By the end of nineteen forty-six, Katsuragi was being cut up for scrap. Once the ferries were no longer needed, the winners had to decide who would keep the rest.

Four Allied powers wanted a share: the United States, Britain, China, and the Soviet Union. China asked for special treatment, because its own navy had been almost wiped out. The solution sounds almost like a game show. The remaining destroyers and smaller warships were split into four equal groups. Each country then drew lots to see which group it would receive.

One of those ships was the destroyer Yukikaze. She had fought in some of the biggest naval battles of the war. Somehow, she survived every one of them. Out of eighty-two Japanese destroyers built before the war, she was one of only three still afloat. In July nineteen forty-seven, she was handed over to China and given a new name, Tan Yang. Her old enemy now flew its flag over her deck.

Another survivor, the destroyer Hibiki, went to the Soviet Union. The Soviets renamed her Verniy, which means faithful. She was faithful to a new owner now. Most of the ships that went to America and Britain had a shorter future. They were not needed, so most of them were simply scrapped.

But some ships were too famous to give away. For those, the Americans had a very different plan.

The battleship Nagato had once been the flagship of the Combined Fleet, Japan's main battle fleet. Admiral Yamamoto had commanded from her decks in the first days of the Pacific war. By the end of the war, she was the last Japanese battleship still afloat.

An American crew took her over and sailed her across the Pacific. Her destination was a tiny ring of islands called Bikini Atoll. There, she became a target for atomic bomb tests. On July first, nineteen forty-six, the first bomb exploded in the air above the anchored fleet. Nagato survived with little damage. Three weeks later, a second bomb exploded underwater. The old battleship stayed afloat for four more days. Then, on the night of July twenty-ninth, she slowly rolled over and sank. Today she lies upside down on the bottom of the lagoon.

So the planes were burned, and the big ships were gone. But one part of the war machine was still out there, waiting in the water.

During the war, Japan had laid tens of thousands of mines to protect its own harbors. American bombers dropped thousands more to strangle Japan's shipping. The mines did not know the war was over. Fishing boats, ferries and cargo ships kept hitting them, and people kept dying.

Somebody had to clear the water. Therefore the Allies kept one small piece of the Japanese navy alive: its minesweepers. Many of the crews were the same men who had served in the Imperial Navy. They went out day after day, dragging cables through cold water. Every sailor knew the next mine could go off right under his own hull. Their work was slow, dangerous, and almost invisible. It was the only part of Japan's war machine that never stopped working.

But soon, Japan would face a much bigger question.

In May nineteen forty-seven, Japan's new constitution came into force. Its ninth article said that Japan gave up war forever. It also said that Japan would never keep land, sea, or air forces. For the first time in its modern history, Japan had no military at all.

Then, on June twenty-fifth, nineteen fifty, North Korea invaded South Korea. The American troops guarding Japan were rushed across the sea to fight. Suddenly, Japan itself was almost undefended.

Within weeks, the occupation authorities ordered a new force called the National Police Reserve, with seventy-five thousand men. It was called a police force, but it was armed and organized much like an army. On land, Japan now had an answer of sorts. The sea was another story.

In October nineteen fifty, United Nations forces planned a landing at Wonsan, a port on North Korea's east coast. But the harbor was full of Soviet-made mines. The Americans did not have enough minesweepers to clear them in time.

So on October second, Rear Admiral Arleigh Burke made a quiet request. He asked Takeo Okubo, the head of Japan's new Maritime Safety Agency, to send minesweepers to Korea. Okubo refused at first, because the new constitution seemed to forbid it. He took the question to Prime Minister Shigeru Yoshida. Yoshida said yes.

On October sixth, sixteen Japanese minesweepers carrying about twelve hundred men left the port of Shimonoseki for Korea. Many of these small ships had been built for the Imperial Navy during the war. Five years earlier, these sailors had been America's enemy. Now they were sweeping mines in front of an American fleet.

Off Wonsan, minesweeper M S fourteen struck a mine and sank. One man was killed, and eighteen were injured. A month later, an American army tugboat with a Japanese crew hit another mine. Twenty-two of its twenty-seven crewmen died.

There was a bitter twist. While the mines were being cleared, South Korean troops had already taken Wonsan by land. When the American landing finally came, the beach was already secure. But the men who died clearing the way did not come back.

Their families were told almost nothing. The whole operation stayed top secret for twenty-nine years, and no medals were ever given.

So what really happened to Japan's war machine? Its planes were stripped of their propellers and burned. Its warships carried millions of people home. After that, they were raffled off, scrapped, or sunk by atomic bombs. But its smallest ships, and the men who sailed them, never really stopped. Five years after the surrender, they were back in a war zone, sweeping mines for their former enemy. In nineteen fifty-four, Japan created its Maritime Self-Defense Force, and the minesweeping crews became part of it. The war machine had been taken apart, piece by piece. But a few of its pieces had simply been waiting.
