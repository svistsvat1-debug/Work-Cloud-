May eighth, nineteen forty-five. The war in Europe is over. You survived it. So you pack your bag and wait for the ride home.

But nobody lets you leave. Because to go home, you need a number. Eighty-five. And you are about to find out if you have it.

Here is the Army's problem. On victory day, about three million Americans were in Europe. Across the world, more than seven million served overseas.

Everyone wanted the first ship home. But there were not nearly enough ships. And the war against Japan still needed men.

Nobody wanted it more than the men themselves. General Maxwell Taylor asked the hundred and first Airborne where they wanted to go next. The men shouted one word. Home.

So who goes first? Back in nineteen forty-three, General George Marshall had put planners on exactly this question.

Their answer was announced on May tenth, nineteen forty-five. Just two days after victory. It had an official name. The Adjusted Service Rating Score.

Soldiers just called it the point system. The idea sounded fair. Those who had fought longest and hardest should go home first.

But there was a catch. Your points were counted only up to May twelfth, nineteen forty-five. Whatever happened to you after that day did not count. At least, not yet.

So let's do what every soldier in Europe did that week. Grab a pencil. Let's count your points.

Rule one. Every month in uniform is worth one point. The count starts in September nineteen forty, when the peacetime draft became law.

Rule two. Every month overseas earns one more point. So a month in England or France is worth double.

Rule three. Every campaign star or combat medal adds five points. That includes the Bronze Star, the Silver Star and the Purple Heart.

Rule four is the one that surprised everyone. Every child under eighteen adds twelve points. Up to three children.

Being married gave you nothing. Your age gave you nothing. But a child back home was worth a full year in uniform. The bar was eighty-five for enlisted men. Eighty-four meant you stayed. Women in the Women's Army Corps needed forty-four.

Cartoonist Bill Mauldin drew a soldier greeting a child as a wonderful little twelve-point rascal.

Now let's play. Meet three soldiers. They are not real people. They are typical cases, built from the real rules.

Card one. The father. A sergeant, thirty years old. Three years in uniform. Two years overseas. Three campaign stars. Two kids at home.

Thirty-six, plus twenty-four, plus fifteen, plus twenty-four. Ninety-nine points. He is going home.

Card two. You. Drafted in January nineteen forty-three. That's twenty-eight months, so twenty-eight points.

You shipped overseas in May nineteen forty-four. Twelve more points. Then you fought from Normandy all the way into Germany.

That's five campaign stars. Twenty-five points. No medals, no kids. Your total is sixty-five.

Card three. The kid. Nineteen years old. Drafted in the summer of nineteen forty-four, he arrived in December, just in time for the Battle of the Bulge.

Ten points for service. Five for months overseas. Fifteen for three campaign stars. Thirty points total.

He was not alone. Private Walter Anderson wrote home. I only have twenty-eight points, so there isn't much chance of me getting home.

So the line was clear. Fathers and long-serving veterans first. Everyone else would wait. But wait for what?

Because the war was not over. Japan was still fighting. And the Army was planning to invade Japan itself.

The first landings were scheduled for November first, nineteen forty-five. The Army needed men for it. Experienced men.

And planners knew the price. A January nineteen forty-five Army study expected about forty-three thousand replacements a month for the dead and wounded.

So every unit in Europe got sorted into four groups. Some would stay as occupation troops. Some would go home and disband.

And some would go to the Pacific. About one million soldiers in Europe were slated for the war against Japan.

Look at your card again. Sixty-five points. Battle-tested, young, no kids. You are exactly the soldier they want.

Then the shuffle began. High-point men were moved into units going home. Low-point men were moved out.

In the Twenty-Eighth Infantry Division, one in five enlisted men changed in a single week. Your squad dissolves around you.

Even the father was not safe. If his skills were needed, the Army could hold him up to six more months.

Boredom set in. Sergeant Gerald Raftery wrote about how monotonous and pointless this whole existence over here has become.

And for one division, the Pacific stopped being a rumor. The Eighty-Sixth Infantry Division sailed home and reached New York on June seventeenth.

They were finally home. But it was only a stopover. The division was being retrained for the war against Japan.

Meanwhile, the high-point men headed for the coast of France. But not straight to a port. To a tent city.

In northern France, the Army ran a string of camps named after cigarette brands. Lucky Strike. Old Gold. Philip Morris. Chesterfield.

They were called the cigarette camps. The brand names were code names, chosen mainly for security.

The biggest, Camp Lucky Strike, could hold fifty-eight thousand soldiers. A city of canvas, waiting for one thing. A ship.

It was also the main stop for freed American prisoners of war. More than seventy-three thousand recovered prisoners passed through Lucky Strike.

Because ships were the real bottleneck. The war against Japan had first claim on them. In May, only ninety thousand soldiers got home.

Everyone else waited for weeks, or months. So the government got creative. It turned three hundred cargo ships into troop transports.

It even got the British liner Queen Mary. The price was ten cargo ships in exchange. The biggest liners squeezed in up to fifteen thousand men.

At Lucky Strike, men got seventy-two hours of warning before departure. After that, nobody could leave the area. But for you, with sixty-five points, there was no warning. And no ship.

Then, in August, everything changed. Two atomic bombs. And on August fifteenth, Japan announced its surrender.

The invasion was cancelled. Overnight, the plan to send a million men from Europe to the Pacific was dead.

But not for the Eighty-Sixth. Nine days after Japan's surrender, the division sailed from San Francisco anyway. Destination, the Philippines.

At home, the pressure exploded. Families wanted their men back now. Wives formed groups called Bring Back Daddy clubs.

Congress was buried in letters. So the Army gave in. Right after victory over Japan, the bar dropped from eighty-five to eighty.

Eisenhower was ordered to ship one million seven hundred sixteen thousand men home by the end of January.

And the Navy joined in. Warships became ferries. Aircraft carriers filled their hangar decks with bunks, three to five beds high.

On September sixth, Admiral Ernest King cleared combat ships to carry troops home. By the autumn, the Atlantic sealift peaked at four hundred thirty thousand soldiers a month.

The new carrier Lake Champlain was fitted with bunks for thirty-three hundred troops. In December, nearly seven hundred thousand came home from the Pacific.

So now the father was gone. The bar was falling. But eighty was still out of reach for your sixty-five.

There was another problem. Somebody had to stay. Germany, Austria and Japan needed occupation troops.

But the draft had been cut to fifty thousand men a month. That was not enough to replace the men leaving.

So on January fourth, nineteen forty-six, the War Department hit the brakes. Discharges would now take six months, not three.

For soldiers already promised a ship, this felt like betrayal. So they did something almost unthinkable. They protested.

On Christmas Day, about four thousand soldiers in Manila protested a cancelled ship. On January sixth, as many as twenty thousand marched on Army headquarters.

The protests spread to Guam, Japan, France, Germany, Austria, India, Korea and England.

In London, about five hundred soldiers confronted Eleanor Roosevelt. In Army mailrooms in Manila and Tokyo, soldiers stamped letters home with a slogan. No boats, no votes.

Protesting in uniform could mean a court-martial. But Eisenhower, now the Army's Chief of Staff, recommended no mass punishment.

His diagnosis was two words. Acute homesickness. He banned further protests. But the Army also sped up the system again.

It also tried to make staying more attractive. Occupation troops could buy a seventeen-day tour of Europe for twenty-five to thirty-five dollars.

So, would you have made it home? Let's run the final count.

The bar kept falling. Seventy by October. Sixty by November. By December nineteenth, it was fifty.

Your sixty-five clears the bar in November. You ride a truck to a cigarette camp. You wait for a ship.

Depending on your ship, you are home that winter. The European part of the sealift was essentially finished by February nineteen forty-six.

The father? He was among the first. Home in the summer, holding his twelve-point kids.

And the kid with thirty points? He never reached the bar. In June nineteen forty-six, the Army scrapped the points entirely.

From then on, two years of service was enough. For the kid, drafted in the summer of nineteen forty-four, that meant the summer of nineteen forty-six.

That's the real answer. Eighty-five was never the finish line. It was a place in a line that kept moving.

The points decided the order. Ships, Japan and the need for occupation troops decided the speed.

The last troops from the Pacific arrived home in September nineteen forty-six. More than a year after the war ended.

In two years, the military shrank from more than twelve million people to about one and a half million.

And every veteran who came home got a small gold-colored pin for the lapel. It showed an eagle. The men thought it looked like a duck. So they called it the Ruptured Duck.
